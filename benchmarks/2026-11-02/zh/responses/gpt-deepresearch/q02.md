# Creatine and Cognitive Function: Evidence vs. Hype

Creatine, a common dietary supplement used to boost muscle energy, has been promoted in the media as enhancing memory and mental performance even in sedentary adults.  To evaluate these claims, we reviewed randomized trials and meta-analyses on creatine and cognition (up to Oct 29, 2026).  Overall, systematic reviews and meta-analyses find **only modest cognitive effects**, largely in specific subgroups (older adults, vegetarians, or sleep-deprived individuals), and **little to no benefit for healthy young adults**.  Headlines often overstate benefits, whereas large reviews and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) have concluded evidence is insufficient to establish a clear cognitive-enhancing effect of creatine. 

## Evidence from Reviews and Meta-Analyses  
Several systematic reviews have assessed creatine’s cognitive effects.  Avgerinos et al. (2018) identified six randomized trials (281 people) and concluded creatine **“may improve short-term memory and intelligence/reasoning”** in healthy individuals, but effects on other domains were mixed.  Notably, they found **young meat-eaters showed no change**, whereas vegetarians showed stronger memory gains.  A 2023 meta-analysis (Prokopidis et al.) pooled eight trials (N≈225) and found a small overall memory benefit (standardized mean difference, SMD≈0.29; 95% CI 0.04–0.53) for creatine versus placebo.  Crucially, this meta-analysis showed **effect modification by age**: older adults (66–76 yrs) had a large memory gain (SMD≈0.88), while younger adults (11–31 yrs) had essentially no effect (SMD≈0.03).  In short, systematic reviews indicate *some* short-term memory/processing gains, especially under stress or in older people, but little evidence of broad cognitive enhancement in healthy young adults.  

A recent review focusing on older adults (55+ years) echoed this pattern: five of six studies showed positive links between creatine and cognition (especially memory and attention), leading the authors to conclude the evidence is *limited but suggestive* of a cognitive benefit in healthy older people.  However, these authors and the EFSA both emphasize that **higher-quality trials are needed**.  The 2024 EFSA opinion explicitly stated that “a cause and effect relationship cannot be established between creatine supplementation and increased cognitive function” due to insufficient consistent evidence.  In summary, expert reviews find *some* cognitive benefit signals – mostly for memory and in certain groups – but **not strong or conclusive evidence** for general use.

## Key Randomized Trials by Subgroup

- **Healthy Young Adults (non-vegetarians):** Most trials in typical young adults report minimal or no cognitive change.  For example, trials cited in Avgerinos (2018) saw no improvement on cognitive tasks for young meat-eaters.  In the Prokopidis (2023) meta, the subgroup of young participants (age 11–31) showed essentially zero effect on memory (SMD≈0.03).  Thus, if you are a healthy 38-year-old without creatine deficiency, trials suggest **little to no benefit** in routine cognitive tests.

- **Vegetarians and Low-Creatine Diets:** Vegetarians naturally have lower creatine intake from diet.  One landmark trial in 2003 (Rae et al.) tested 45 healthy vegetarian adults: they gave 5 g/day creatine for six weeks and found significant improvements in *working memory* (backward digit span) and *reasoning* (Raven’s matrices) versus placebo (p<0.0001).  This suggests that in vegetarians – who start with lower brain creatine levels – supplementation can boost cognitive processing speed.  Avgerinos et al. (2018) also noted that vegetarians **responded better than omnivores** on memory tasks.  However, it’s worth noting that not all studies of vegetarians replicate such large effects, and these gains were measured on specific tests.  In everyday life the practical impact of, say, slightly faster recall or reasoning is uncertain.  

- **Older Adults:** While our focus is on a 38-year-old, it’s informative that creatine’s cognitive effects appear largest in older populations.  The Prokopidis meta found a **large improvement** in memory tests for adults 66–76 (SMD≈0.88).  The systematic review of older adults likewise reported generally positive effects on memory/attention.  This age-related benefit may reflect lower baseline creatine (both dietary and endogenous) in elders, making supplementation more impactful.  For a younger person, the evidence implies far smaller gains.

- **Sleep-Deprived Individuals:** A handful of studies suggest creatine may offset some cognitive deficits from sleep loss.  In one recent trial (Gordji-Nejad et al. 2024), 15 healthy volunteers underwent ~21 hours of wakefulness.  Subjects received either a **single high dose** of creatine (about 0.3 g/kg, roughly 20 g for a 70 kg person) or placebo.  The creatine group showed maintained brain energy levels and *improved cognitive performance* (processing capacity and short-term memory) compared to placebo.  In other words, creatine **partially protected against sleep-deprivation-induced declines**.  Earlier work (Cook et al. 2011) similarly found that acute creatine helped psychomotor skills under sleep loss.  These findings suggest that, if you are severely sleep-deprived on occasion, taking creatine might *partially* mitigate the impact on tasks like reaction time or working memory.  However, these are small, short-term studies – not evidence of routine cognitive boosting – and often use a *very large dose at once*. 

## Size of the Improvements

When gains are reported, they tend to be **modest**.  The Prokopidis meta’s overall SMD of ~0.29 for memory corresponds to a small-to-moderate effect size.  For context, an SMD of 0.3 might translate to a few percentile points higher on memory tests – statistically significant but not a dramatic change.  In everyday terms, this might mean remembering a couple extra items on a list or solving a few more puzzles correctly, which many users might not notice as a big difference.  Notably, that meta reported **no meaningful effect** in younger adults (SMD essentially zero), so the average improvement in a 38-year-old healthy person would likely be negligible.  

By contrast, the older subgroup’s SMD≈0.88 is a larger effect: in research settings that could mean much faster recall or solving considerably more problems correctly.  But even that applies to much older adults.  The take-home is that **young, non-vegetarian adults see at best very small cognitive changes**, whereas any headlines suggesting dramatic memory boosts are not supported by the data.

## Doses Studied and Safety

Studies have used a wide range of creatine dosing.  Many trials use a standard regimen (e.g. 20 g/day split dosing for 5–7 days loading, then ~5 g/day maintenance).  The Prokopidis meta noted no effect of dose or duration within the studied range (roughly 2.2–20 g/day for 5 days up to 24 weeks) on outcomes.  The sleep-deprivation study used a single ~0.3 g/kg dose (around 20 g) at once.  If one were to try creatine, a reasonable approach (mirroring muscle-health studies) is a loading phase (e.g. 15–20 g/day for a few days) followed by ~3–5 g/day maintenance.

Creatine is generally **well-tolerated** in healthy adults.  Extensive research in athletes and patients shows few serious adverse effects at these doses.  Minor side effects can include weight gain (from water retention in muscles) and gastrointestinal upset in some people, but no evidence of kidney damage or long-term harm in healthy individuals.  (Note: people with existing kidney disease or other conditions should consult a doctor before supplementing.)  In cognitive studies specifically, no major safety issues have been reported.  The EFSA and other bodies have not raised new safety concerns for creatine use at typical supplementation levels.

## Remaining Uncertainties

Important questions remain.  Most trials are small (tens of participants) and short-term; larger, longer trials are lacking.  The optimal dosing for brain uptake is unclear – creatine crosses into the brain slowly, so some suggest that high single doses (as in the sleep study) or loading phases may be needed.  Differences in baseline diet and muscle mass (e.g. strict vegetarians versus omnivores) likely affect how much creatine raises brain levels.  The EFSA noted that mechanistic evidence (how creatine would work in the brain) is incomplete.  There is also publication bias risk: positive findings are more likely to be published or publicized.  In healthy, well-rested people, the 2024 review mentioned above found *no significant cognitive benefit*.  

Finally, even where statistical improvements occur, it’s uncertain whether they translate to meaningful everyday benefits.  Testing cognitive “performance” can pick up small differences under controlled conditions, but that might not change daily productivity or memory in a noticeable way.

## Conclusion

In summary, *current human evidence offers only weak support for routine cognitive enhancement by creatine in a healthy 38-year-old*.  Some randomized trials and reviews find **small improvements in memory/processing**, especially in special cases: older adults, vegetarians (who start with lower creatine), or people under sleep-deprived/stressful conditions.  For a typical young vegetarian office worker, benefits are likely to be modest at best.  Headlines claiming “creatine boosts brain” overlook that most healthy young subjects showed no effect, and that meta-analyses report only minor test-score gains.  

If one chooses to try creatine (e.g. 5 g daily), it is probably safe and might marginally help short-term memory tasks (especially if normally fatigued or short on sleep).  But it should not be seen as a guaranteed cognitive “smart drug.”  More large-scale trials are needed to determine definitively who might benefit and under what conditions.  For now, any cognitive upside appears limited, and expectations should be tempered accordingly.
