### 2.2. Value per kWh shifted under your assumed flat tariff

<strong>With 27p import and 15p export, each kWh of solar that you move from export to self‑consumption is worth \~12p before battery losses; battery inefficiencies eat part of that, leaving roughly 10–11p net.</strong>

Mechanism:

- Without a battery, 1 kWh of surplus solar exported earns 15p.
- With a battery:

  - That 1 kWh charges the battery; with 90% round‑trip efficiency you later get 0.9 kWh useful AC.
  - You avoid importing 0.9 kWh at 27p/kWh = 24.3p.
  - You forgo the 15p SEG you would have been paid.
  - Incremental benefit ≈ 24.3p − 15p = <strong>9.3p per kWh of <em>discharged</em> energy</strong>.
  - Expressed per original stored kWh, it’s the same (24.3p imported value divided by 0.9 ≈ 27p; 27p − 15p = 12p, but only 90% of that emerges as AC).

To keep the analysis simple and conservative, it’s reasonable to say:

- Each kWh of <em>battery discharge</em> that would otherwise have been exported solar creates roughly <strong>9–12p of net value</strong>, depending on exact efficiencies and tariff details.

The decisive point is that this value per shifted kWh is modest because your export rate (15p) is already high: you are not moving energy from “free” spill to the grid, you are trading export income.

### 3.2. Net present value and degradation

<strong>Once you factor in degradation, discounting, and the 10‑year warranty, the net present value (NPV) of a 5 kWh or 10 kWh battery used only for solar shifting on flat tariffs is almost certainly negative.</strong>

Mechanisms working against positive NPV:

- <strong>Degradation</strong>: captured value per year declines over time because the battery holds and delivers less energy. A linear drop from 100% to 70% usable capacity over 10 years implies an average of only \~85% of year‑1 earnings across the warranty period.
- <strong>Discounting</strong>: even modest real discount rates (e.g. 3–5%) shrink the present value of savings occurring 7–15 years in the future.
- <strong>End‑of‑warranty risk</strong>: after 10 years you are outside the warranty, and the economic rationality of replacing the battery then depends on future cost and tariff trajectories.

A realistic NPV assessment with:

- £4,500 upfront for 5 kWh,
- year‑1 incremental saving \~£150, decaying with degradation, and
- 10 years of warranted life<br>
   would almost certainly show discounted cumulative benefits well under £4,500 unless you assume sustained, substantial increases in the import‑export price spread.

## 5 4. Impact of Time‑of‑Use and Off‑Peak Tariffs

Time‑of‑use (ToU) tariffs fundamentally change battery economics by turning storage from a “SEG‑spread” product into a “wholesale price spread” product.

### 4.1. Off‑peak import for self‑use (Economy 7 / EV‑style tariffs)

<strong>Standard Economy 7‑type tariffs in 2026 offer night rates around 9–14p/kWh versus day rates around 27–34p/kWh[\[33\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/news/october-energy-price-cap-to-increase-by-four-percent/> "Economy 7 rates under Q4 2026 price cap")[\[34\]](<https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/what-is-economy-7-is-it-cheaper-aF1aS4w0y0xP> "Economy 7 guidance on required off-peak share"), while EV/Intelligent tariffs can drop off‑peak import to \~5–7p/kWh[\[35\]](<https://octopus.energy/intelligent-go/> "Intelligent Octopus Go off-peak rates")[\[36\]](<https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/utilities/cheap-ev-tariffs/> "Off-peak EV tariff examples (So Energy, EDF)")[\[37\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/electric-car/charging-at-home/> "Off-peak EV tariffs dropping to ~5.5p/kWh"). A battery lets you capture that spread even without shifting your physical usage.</strong>

Mechanism:

- Charge battery from the grid overnight at the cheaper rate; discharge during the day/evening to avoid imports at the higher rate.
- Discharge energy then effectively earns you (day rate − night rate) per kWh, minus losses.

Example using representative Q4 2026 numbers:

- Night: say 14p/kWh; Day: say 30–34p/kWh[\[33\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/news/october-energy-price-cap-to-increase-by-four-percent/> "Economy 7 rates under Q4 2026 price cap").

  - Spread ≈ 16–20p/kWh.
- With 90% round‑trip efficiency, every kWh of <em>discharge</em> avoids \~30–34p import; you paid 14p for \~1.11 kWh charged. Net ≈ 30–34p − 14p ≈ <strong>16–20p per discharged kWh</strong>.

EV‑oriented tariffs can look even better:

- Intelligent Octopus‑style deals offer off‑peak \~5.5–9.4p/kWh overnight[\[35\]](<https://octopus.energy/intelligent-go/> "Intelligent Octopus Go off-peak rates")[\[36\]](<https://www.moneysavingexpert.com/utilities/cheap-ev-tariffs/> "Off-peak EV tariff examples (So Energy, EDF)"), with on‑peak around or above the price cap (low‑30p region).
- That can create spreads of 20p+/kWh.

If your battery cycles primarily on ToU arbitrage rather than solar shifting, the incremental value per discharged kWh doubles or triples compared with the 9–12p you get from “27p vs 15p” solar shifting. That is why many 2026 payback examples showing 10–15 year ROI explicitly assume ToU schemes[\[18\]](<https://www.smartsolarcompare.online/solar-savings-guide> "5kWh battery payback and annual savings")[\[23\]](<https://www.theecoexperts.co.uk/solar-panels/solar-battery-storage> "Solar battery adds £200–£350/year savings")[\[27\]](<https://www.fmb.org.uk/homepicks/solar-panels/are-solar-batteries-worth-it/> "FMB solar battery payback analysis (10.6 years)").

### 4.2. Solar‑plus‑ToU stacking and export arbitrage (Flux/Agile‑style)

<strong>Dynamic or tri‑rate tariffs like Octopus Flux or Agile introduce peak export prices that can strongly favour a larger battery used for both import arbitrage and “charge‑cheap, export‑dear” strategies.</strong>

- Flux‑type tariffs have Bristol examples with peak import \~38.6p/kWh, off‑peak import \~16.6p/kWh, and corresponding export prices (peak export \~29.2p/kWh, off‑peak export \~4.6p/kWh)[\[38\]](<https://octopus.energy/smart/octopus-flux/> "Octopus Flux Bristol tariff example").
- More generally, ToU export tariffs pay highest rates in early evening (4–7pm) when grid demand peaks and PV generation is low[\[39\]](<https://greenhatrenewables.co.uk/blog/seg-rates-2026-best-export-tariff/> "Solar battery tariffs and peak export windows").

With a battery, you can:

- maximise self‑consumption;
- charge cheaply overnight or during low‑price periods; and
- optionally export at the peak export window if your battery and PV are both full or you don’t need the energy later.

A specific minority analysis using Octopus Flux Bristol numbers concluded:

- <strong>Under Flux</strong>, a 10 kWh battery in your sort of scenario produces a <em>positive</em> NPV of around £1,100 over 10 years, while a 5 kWh battery is roughly breakeven[\[30\]](<https://reonic.com/en-gb/blog/solar-battery-tariffs/> "Octopus Flux and storage economics in Bristol (2026)").
- That study leveraged both import and export spreads; the 10 kWh size allowed enough throughput in peak hours to exploit the tariff differential, whereas the 5 kWh capacity constrained daily arbitrage.

Qualitatively, this makes sense:

- On flat tariffs, a bigger battery suffers diminishing returns because PV generation caps usable cycles.
- On sharply peaky tariffs, additional capacity in the evening peak window directly scales arbitrage revenue up to the point your PV and/or off‑peak import limit is reached.

However, these gains are contingent on:

- you actually being allowed onto those tariffs with your existing system and without an EV;
- tariff structures and spreads remaining favourable over a large part of the battery’s life;
- active management (manual or automated) to avoid “getting it wrong” on volatile days.

### 4.3. ToU tariffs: when do they materially change your decision?

<strong>ToU or EV‑style off‑peak tariffs can transform the economics from “clearly negative NPV” to “borderline to moderately positive NPV”, but only if you are comfortable with complexity and some tariff risk.</strong>

Conditions under which ToU <em>does</em> materially change the answer:

- You secure a tariff with:

  - off‑peak import at or below \~7–10p/kWh; and
  - on‑peak import/ex­port rates close to or above current price‑cap levels (\~26–30p/kWh for imports in Q4 2026[\[40\]](<https://www.britishgas.co.uk/energy/price-cap.html> "British Gas – October 2026 price cap unit rates")[\[41\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/news/october-energy-price-cap-to-increase-by-four-percent/> "Uswitch – October 2026 price cap figures")).
- Your battery cycles most days of the year, with typical per‑day throughput of:

  - \~3–4 kWh for a 5 kWh unit;
  - \~6–8 kWh for a 10 kWh unit.
- The tariff design or your automation (e.g. a smart inverter app) avoids many instances of “charging high, discharging low” that would harm arbitrage returns.

If these hold, the incremental per‑kWh value (16–20p) times daily throughput can plausibly generate £300–£500/year or more, which brings payback into the low‑teens for a 5 kWh and into the teens for a 10 kWh, <em>even before solar‑only benefits</em>. That is the regime where adding a battery begins to look financially rational on a 10‑year horizon.

Conditions where ToU <em>does not</em> help enough:

- Off‑peak discount is shallow (e.g. 22p vs 27p).
- You don’t fully exploit off‑peak windows because your home load is modest and you cap discharge to preserve battery longevity.
- You cannot or do not want to pursue active tariff management, and default automations are conservative.

## 6 5. Policy, VAT, Financing and Non‑financial Factors

### 5.1. VAT and financing

<strong>Several UK policy features marginally improve the numbers but do not fundamentally change the picture at your volumes unless combined with ToU upside.</strong>

For a stand‑alone battery quote that currently includes zero VAT, this doesn’t <em>improve</em> things further, but it does mean that delaying beyond early 2027 will make batteries slightly more expensive.
- A recently announced “Warm Homes Loan Scheme” will offer interest‑free or low‑interest loans of £7,500–£35,000 for solar, batteries, heat pumps and similar technologies[\[44\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/news/warm-homes-loan-scheme-to-cut-energy-bills> "Warm Homes Loan Scheme announcement")[\[45\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/warm-homes-loan-scheme-overview> "Warm Homes Loan details: loan sizes and technologies").

  - This reduces or eliminates the financing cost of the upfront capital, but it does <strong>not</strong> by itself make the project positive NPV: it mainly spreads cashflow.
  - It can, however, make a borderline‑economical battery more attractive if your alternative use for the capital is low‑yield (e.g. cash savings).

### 5.2. Battery durability and warranty risk

<strong>Long nominal lifespans (10–15 years) are offset by degradation and manufacturer risk, so relying on “it will last 15–20 years, therefore payback is fine” is unsafe.</strong>

- Typical warranties: 10 years, 70% capacity, 6,000+ cycles[\[7\]](<https://solarlove.org/solar-battery-costs-guide/> "Home battery warranties and degradation")[\[9\]](<https://batteryblueprint.com/payback-reality/> "UK industry-standard battery warranty terms")[\[46\]](<https://www.homebuilding.co.uk/advice/solar-battery-storage> "Solar battery warranty norms 2026"). Beyond this, degradation, possible inverter obsolescence, and manufacturer failures (as seen with GivEnergy’s 2026 administration event, after which warranties and app support were not honoured[\[47\]](<https://www.solarpowerportal.co.uk/news/givenergy-placed-into-administration> "GivEnergy administration and warranty issues (April 2026)")) all introduce risk.
- From a financial perspective, you should discount any projections beyond 10–12 years heavily; the case really needs to work on that timescale.

### 5.3. Non‑financial benefits

<strong>Several non‑financial benefits might matter to you even if the pure NPV case is weak.</strong>

- <strong>Resilience / backup</strong>: some systems can provide backup during power cuts. If you place high value on continuity (home office, medical devices), that has subjective worth not captured in tariff arithmetic.
- <strong>Carbon optimisation</strong>: ToU + battery lets you shift consumption away from high‑carbon peak generation (often fossil‑heavy) to off‑peak periods with more wind. The climate benefit per £ invested is hard to benchmark but is real.
- <strong>Future‑proofing</strong>: if you expect to install an EV charger or heat pump within a few years, sizing and wiring for future storage and ToU optimisation may avoid costly retrofits later.

These factors can make it reasonable to choose a battery with negative or borderline financial returns, but they should be consciously weighed against the £ cost.

Your question explicitly asks which conclusions are robust at your stated annual/average numbers and which depend critically on half‑hourly usage and generation.

### 6.1. Conclusions that follow robustly from the given annual numbers

The following points hold with high confidence given only your aggregate data and today’s UK context:

1. <strong>Export value is already high relative to import, so solar‑only storage is weakly justified.</strong>

    

   - With 15p/kWh SEG, the “spread” a battery can exploit (27p−15p) is small and fixed; even full utilisation of a 5 kWh battery only yields \~£100–£180/year of extra net value from solar shifting.
2. <strong>A 10 kWh battery is over‑sized for a 4 kWp array and 4,500 kWh/year household under flat tariffs.</strong>

    

   - Your PV only produces 3,600 kWh/year. Even heroic self‑consumption (e.g. 90%) tops out at \~3,240 kWh; it is hard to justify paying almost twice the capital cost of a 5 kWh system to capture the marginal few hundred extra kWh.
3. <strong>Under current 2026 UK pricing, stand‑alone battery payback for your situation is longer than 10 years unless ToU arbitrage is added.</strong>

    

   - Industry and independent numbers converge on \~10–15 years for <em>favourable</em> cases with ToU or EV loads[\[17\]](<https://topgreendeals.co.uk/solar-calculator> "5kWh battery savings and self-consumption increase")[\[18\]](<https://www.smartsolarcompare.online/solar-savings-guide> "5kWh battery payback and annual savings")[\[27\]](<https://www.fmb.org.uk/homepicks/solar-panels/are-solar-batteries-worth-it/> "FMB solar battery payback analysis (10.6 years)")[\[31\]](<https://www.thecooldown.com/green-home/solar-battery-installation-uk-homeowners-payback/> "UK homeowner solar battery installation payback story"). Removing ToU and EV but keeping similar capital costs pushes simple payback well beyond warranty length.
4. <strong>Upgrading your export tariff is likely a higher‑return, lower‑risk move than adding storage right now.</strong>

    

   - As of mid‑2026, SEG rates vary widely (from under 1p to around 20p/kWh; several suppliers in the 12–15p range[\[25\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/solar-panels/guides/smart-export-guarantee/> "Smart Export Guarantee rates 2026 UK")[\[26\]](<https://kilowattkit.com/tools/solar/seg-calculator> "SEG export rate comparison 2026")[\[48\]](<https://spectrumenergysystems.co.uk/articles/solar-export-tariff-comparison-2026/> "SEG export tariff comparison 2026 (best rates)")). Ensuring you are at the top end of that range on a simple export contract yields assured extra income at virtually no capital cost.

### 6.2. Conclusions that depend heavily on half‑hourly consumption and solar profiles

Several more nuanced decisions <em>do</em> depend on half‑hourly data or at least a realistic load/generation profile:

1. <strong>Optimal battery size (if any) under ToU tariffs.</strong>

    

   - A 5 kWh battery may be optimal for many 4 kWp systems without EVs, but if your evening peak is unusually high (e.g. electric cooking, immersion heaters), a 10 kWh unit could exploit more ToU arbitrage and approach positive NPV under Flux‑like tariffs.
   - Conversely, if your evening load is modest and spread across the day, even a 5 kWh unit may be under‑utilised.
2. <strong>Realistic annual cycling rate.</strong>

    

   - Annual averages can suggest “one cycle per day”, but winter output in Bristol is low; actual cycles will drop in the dark months. Detailed modelling of PV output by month[\[2\]](<https://solarenergyconcepts.co.uk/system-guides/solar-panel-output-by-month-uk/> "Solar panel output by month in the UK")[\[49\]](<https://solar4good.com/bristol-solar-output-guide> "Solar output Bristol 4 kW – seasonal shape") and your own load shape strongly influences achievable kWh throughput and hence £ savings.
3. <strong>Degree of overlap between PV generation and load without a battery.</strong>

    

   - You report “mostly evening” use, but if in practice you run sizeable daytime loads (e.g. home office, daytime cooking), your self‑consumption may already be nearer 40–45% in summer. That narrows the extra headroom a battery can fill.
4. <strong>Sensitivity to different export/import tariff permutations.</strong>

    

   - With half‑hourly data and real tariff time slices (e.g. Flux’s 4–7 pm peak band, Agile’s half‑hour prices), you can simulate dispatch strategies and compute precise arbitrage revenue. That is necessary to judge whether the more optimistic “10 kWh under Flux is worthwhile” conclusion applies to your specific pattern.

## 8 7. Scenario Analysis

Bringing all dimensions together, here are three structured scenarios for you:

### Scenario A – Stay on flat tariff, keep current export arrangement (Status Quo+)

- Actions: no battery; ensure you are on a high SEG rate (e.g. 13–20p/kWh where accessible[\[25\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/solar-panels/guides/smart-export-guarantee/> "Smart Export Guarantee rates 2026 UK")[\[48\]](<https://spectrumenergysystems.co.uk/articles/solar-export-tariff-comparison-2026/> "SEG export tariff comparison 2026 (best rates)")).
- Economics:

  - No new capital; you continue to save from PV plus export.
  - Upside from tweaking SEG is on the order of £50–£150/year compared with mediocre export offers, at zero capex[\[26\]](<https://kilowattkit.com/tools/solar/seg-calculator> "SEG export rate comparison 2026").
- When it’s optimal:

  - You are risk‑averse on capital projects;
  - You’re satisfied with existing bill levels;
  - You have no near‑term plans for EV/heat pump.

### Scenario B – Add \~5 kWh battery, stay essentially on flat tariffs

- Actions: install a 5 kWh battery; keep a high fixed import tariff and reasonable export rate (\~15p).
- Economics (indicative):

  - Capital \~£4,000–£5,000; incremental net solar‑shifting benefit \~£100–£180/year.
  - Simple payback 22–50 years; NPV likely negative on a 10–12 year horizon.
- Non‑financial:

  - You gain some backup capacity (if the system supports it), modest carbon benefits from increasing self‑consumption, and “feel‑good” self‑reliance.
- When it might still appeal:

  - You place high subjective value on resilience/climate impact;
  - You have access to a subsidised or interest‑free loan and low alternative uses for the cash.

### Scenario C – Add 5–10 kWh battery and move to ToU / Flux‑like tariff

- Actions:

  - Install 5 or 10 kWh battery;
  - Move to a ToU tariff (Economy 7 / Cosy / Flux / Agile) that you are eligible for;
  - Use automation (or discipline) to charge during cheap windows and discharge during expensive ones, while also harnessing your PV.
- Economics (indicative):

  - Potential incremental savings from ToU arbitrage plus solar shifting could rise into the £300–£500+/year range for 5 kWh, higher for 10 kWh in the right pattern[\[18\]](<https://www.smartsolarcompare.online/solar-savings-guide> "5kWh battery payback and annual savings")[\[20\]](<https://topgreendeals.co.uk/solar-calculator> "5 kWh battery raises solar share from 39% to 72%")[\[27\]](<https://www.fmb.org.uk/homepicks/solar-panels/are-solar-batteries-worth-it/> "FMB solar battery payback analysis (10.6 years)")[\[30\]](<https://reonic.com/en-gb/blog/solar-battery-tariffs/> "Octopus Flux and storage economics in Bristol (2026)").
  - A Bristol‑specific Flux analysis suggested 10 kWh could achieve a modestly positive NPV (\~£1,100 over 10 years), with 5 kWh borderline around break‑even[\[30\]](<https://reonic.com/en-gb/blog/solar-battery-tariffs/> "Octopus Flux and storage economics in Bristol (2026)").
- When this is attractive:

  - You’re willing to engage with more complex tariffs and accept regulatory/tariff evolution risk;
  - You expect consistent or rising import prices and durable off‑peak spreads;
  - You are open to using smart automation and occasionally adjusting your usage around price signals.

## 9 8. Recommendations

### Immediate (0–6 months)

1. <strong>Optimise your present export and import tariffs.</strong>

    

   - Confirm your current SEG rate; if below the \~13–20p/kWh range available in 2026[\[25\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/solar-panels/guides/smart-export-guarantee/> "Smart Export Guarantee rates 2026 UK")[\[48\]](<https://spectrumenergysystems.co.uk/articles/solar-export-tariff-comparison-2026/> "SEG export tariff comparison 2026 (best rates)"), switch supplier or product first.
   - Similarly, ensure your flat import rate around 27p/kWh is competitive given the Ofgem price cap of about 26.3p/kWh in Q4 2026[\[40\]](<https://www.britishgas.co.uk/energy/price-cap.html> "British Gas – October 2026 price cap unit rates")[\[41\]](<https://www.uswitch.com/gas-electricity/news/october-energy-price-cap-to-increase-by-four-percent/> "Uswitch – October 2026 price cap figures").
2. <strong>Collect and inspect your half‑hourly smart‑meter data.</strong>

    

   - Obtain at least 12 months of half‑hourly imports and exports if possible.
   - Plot typical winter vs summer day profiles. This will quantify:

     - actual daytime vs evening use;
     - how often, and by how much, PV exceeds your load;
     - typical peak‑demand windows.

This step is essential to move from generic modelling to a personal ROI estimate.

### Short‑term (6–18 months)

1. <strong>Run a personalised battery and tariff simulation before committing.</strong>

    

   - Use an advanced calculator that accepts half‑hourly or hourly data, or work with a reputable installer or independent consultant who can run simulations using your measured profiles and realistic tariff choices (including Flux/Agile).
   - Require that any quote includes:

     - assumed number of cycles per year;
     - explicit import/export tariff scenarios;
     - degradation assumptions;
     - 10‑year cash‑flow and NPV estimates.
2. <strong>If you want storage primarily for self‑consumption and resilience, favour a 5 kWh system over 10 kWh.</strong>

    

   - On flat tariffs, 5 kWh is the only capacity with any plausible route towards long‑term break‑even in your context; 10 kWh is effectively a lifestyle/resilience purchase.
   - Even if you later adopt ToU, a 5 kWh unit is easier to keep heavily utilised with your present load and PV size.

### Medium‑term (18–36 months)

1. <strong>Monitor ToU and policy developments up to the 0% VAT deadline in March 2027.</strong>

    

   - Keep an eye on:

     - the evolution of EV/ToU tariffs for non‑EV households;
     - any adjustments to SEG and dynamic export products;
     - the implementation details of the Warm Homes Loan Scheme in England.
   - If by 2027 ToU spreads remain wide and you have robust personalised modelling showing a 5 kWh (or, less likely, 10 kWh) battery achieving payback within \~10–12 years, consider investing before VAT rises and while policy‑backed loans are available.
2. <strong>Re‑evaluate if you add an EV or a heat pump.</strong>

    

   - Both materially increase electricity consumption and evening load. In that case:

     - The daily cycle count for a battery rises;
     - Solar self‑consumption potential increases;
     - EV‑specific tariffs become accessible.
   - Under that changed load, a larger battery (7–10 kWh) can become substantially more economic than it is today.

## 10 9. Bottom Line for Your Decision Today

- With <strong>4 kWp PV, 4,500 kWh/year evening‑heavy load, and 27p/15p flat tariffs</strong>, <strong>neither a 5 kWh nor a 10 kWh battery presently offers a compelling financial return if used only to store excess solar</strong>.
- <strong>A 5 kWh unit</strong> is closer to viability and might approximately break even over 10–15 years under optimistic assumptions, but still carries non‑trivial downside risk and relies heavily on future tariff evolution.
- <strong>A 10 kWh unit</strong> looks uneconomic in purely financial terms in your current configuration; it only makes sense either:

  - as a deliberate resilience / lifestyle choice; or
  - if paired with aggressive ToU arbitrage and, ideally, future incremental loads (EV, heat pump).

Under the constraints you specified, the rational course is therefore:

> <strong>Keep your current setup, optimise tariffs, gather half‑hourly data, and only commit to a battery if ToU opportunities and/or your load shape clearly shift the numbers into the 10–15‑year payback range on a properly modelled NPV basis.</strong>
