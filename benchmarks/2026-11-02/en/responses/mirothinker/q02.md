## 2 1. What Meta‑Analyses and Systematic Reviews Actually Find

<strong>Key insight:</strong> Pooled data support <em>domain‑specific</em>, small cognitive benefits—mainly memory and processing speed—without improvement in global cognition, and these benefits cluster in “stressed” groups (older age, illness, possible low creatine).

### 1.1 Overall adult population

A 2024 meta‑analysis in <em>Frontiers in Nutrition</em> pooled 16 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) including 492 adults and found that creatine monohydrate supplementation[\[2\]](<https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full> "Xu T, et al. Creatine monohydrate supplementation on cognitive function in adults: A systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized controlled trials"):

- <strong>Significantly improved memory</strong> with a standardized mean difference (SMD) of 0.31 (95% CI 0.18–0.44).
- <strong>Improved attention/processing speed</strong> (also statistically significant).
- <strong>Did not significantly improve global cognitive function or executive function.</strong>

An earlier quantitative synthesis reported very similar results: <strong>overall memory SMD about 0.29</strong>, with a <strong>much larger apparent effect in older adults (SMD ≈ 0.88 from only two studies)</strong> and essentially <strong>no effect in younger adults (SMD ≈ 0.03)</strong>.[\[1\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Prokopidis K, et al. “Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance.” (details summarized in review and meta‑analysis)")

A 2026 narrative review and related analyses converge on the same pattern: <strong>modest improvements in memory, processing speed and sometimes executive function, particularly in people with lower baseline creatine levels</strong> (older adults, possibly vegetarians, some clinical groups).[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[8\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11820685/> "Pratt J, et al. Creatine for brain health and cognition: current evidence and perspectives")

Mechanistically, this fits the idea that creatine primarily supports ATP buffering during <strong>metabolic stress</strong> (aging, hypoxia, sleep loss, neurological disease). In well‑rested young omnivores with full brain stores, extra creatine has little room to help.

### 1.2 Older adults and aging

A 2026 <em>Nutrition Reviews</em> systematic review focused on older adults (≥55 years; 6 studies, N≈1,500) concluded that <strong>5 of 6 studies reported a positive relationship between creatine and cognition</strong>, particularly for memory and attention/processing speed.[\[9\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40971619/> "Marshall AC, et al. Creatine and cognition in aging: a systematic review") The authors cautioned that the trials were heterogeneous and often combined creatine with exercise or other interventions, but still summarized that <strong>creatine “may be associated with benefits” for cognition in generally healthy older adults</strong>.[\[9\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40971619/> "Marshall AC, et al. Creatine and cognition in aging: a systematic review")

A separate 2026 review similarly notes that benefits appear <strong>larger and more consistent in elderly individuals and in those with lower baseline creatine or neurodegenerative risk</strong>, again mainly on memory and psychomotor speed.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")

### 1.3 Skeptical reviews

By contrast, a 2024 systematic review by McMorris concluded that although creatine reliably increases brain creatine content, the <strong>results for cognitive outcomes are “somewhat equivocal” and do not robustly support the theoretical basis for a general cognitive benefit</strong>.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review") The review highlighted:

- <strong>Null or minimal effects in healthy young adults</strong>, including large, well‑designed RCTs.
- Stronger, but still inconsistent, signals in specific groups (vegetarians, elderly, sleep‑deprived, hypoxic).

This skeptical stance is one reason <strong>regulatory agencies did not grant health claims</strong>, despite the statistically significant findings in meta‑analyses (see Section 4).

<strong>Implication for you:</strong> Meta‑analyses support a <strong>small, statistically clear improvement in memory and processing speed</strong>, but <strong>not a broad “smarter brain” effect</strong>, and the strongest pooled effects come from populations older than you or under greater physiologic stress.

## 3 2. What the Individual Randomized Trials Show in Populations Relevant to You

To ground the meta‑analytic picture, it helps to look at the RCTs closest to your situation: vegetarians, young/middle‑aged adults, and sleep‑deprived individuals.

### 2.1 Vegetarian and low‑creatine diets

#### 2.1.1 Early positive trials

The most famous trial for vegetarian cognition is Rae et al. 2003, a <strong>double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover RCT in 45 young vegetarian/vegan adults</strong> given <strong>5 g/day creatine for six weeks</strong>.[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")[\[11\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005770/> "Rae C, Digney AL, McEwan SR, Bates TC. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves memory and intelligence in vegetarian young adults")

- Creatine significantly improved <strong>working memory (backward digit span)</strong> and <strong>performance on Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices</strong> (a fluid intelligence test).[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")[\[11\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005770/> "Rae C, Digney AL, McEwan SR, Bates TC. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves memory and intelligence in vegetarian young adults")
- The working memory gain was large in statistical terms within that small trial (roughly an extra digit in backward span).[\[11\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005770/> "Rae C, Digney AL, McEwan SR, Bates TC. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves memory and intelligence in vegetarian young adults")

Benton &amp; Donohoe 2011 examined 121 female undergraduates, including vegetarians and omnivores, with a <strong>5‑day 20 g/day loading protocol</strong>. They reported that[\[12\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21118604/> "Benton D, Donohoe RT. The influence of creatine supplementation on the cognitive functioning of vegetarians and omnivores"):

- <strong>Vegetarians improved on memory measures</strong>,
- <strong>Omnivores did not</strong>,
- Effect sizes were modest (partial η² around 0.04–0.05).

These two early trials generated the narrative that <strong>vegetarians, because they consume little or no dietary creatine, get disproportionate cognitive benefit</strong>.

#### 2.1.2 Newer, larger, more neutral data

Later work complicates this story.

- A 2023 preregistered, double‑blind crossover RCT (Sandkühler et al.) in <strong>123 young adults (about half vegetarian)</strong> used <strong>5 g/day for 6 weeks</strong>, then crossover. This is the largest cognitive creatine trial to date. It <strong>did not find meaningful improvements in overall cognitive performance</strong> and <strong>did not confirm a clear vegetarian‑specific benefit</strong>.[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- A 2020 systematic review on creatine and plant‑based diets reported that <strong>brain creatine concentrations appear similar between vegetarians and omnivores</strong>, despite large differences in dietary intake, and that data were <strong>mixed on whether vegetarians gain more cognitive benefit</strong>.[\[14\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets")
- A 2020 experiment giving <strong>0.3 g/kg/day creatine for 7 days to vegetarians and omnivores</strong> failed to show an increase in brain phosphocreatine in either group (measured by 31P‑MRS), again challenging the assumption of large brain creatine deficits in vegetarians.[\[15\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine supplementation and brain phosphocreatine in vegetarians and omnivores")

Recent commentaries and reviews now tend to frame the “vegetarian advantage” as <strong>possible but unproven</strong>: earlier positive studies exist, but <strong>larger and more mechanistically detailed work does not consistently show lower baseline brain creatine or larger cognitive gains</strong> in vegetarians vs omnivores.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review")[\[14\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets")[\[15\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine supplementation and brain phosphocreatine in vegetarians and omnivores")

<strong>Relevance for you:</strong> As a vegetarian, you are slightly more likely to benefit than a meat‑eater if a benefit exists, but <strong>you should not expect the large effect sizes of early small trials</strong>. The best modern data suggest any advantage is <strong>small and not guaranteed</strong>.

### 2.2 Healthy young and middle‑aged adults (your age band)

Your age (38) falls well within the “young‑to‑middle‑aged adult” strata where evidence is <strong>weakest</strong>.

- In the age‑stratified meta‑analysis, <strong>younger adults (up to about 60) showed near‑zero pooled memory effect (SMD ≈ 0.03)</strong>, whereas older adults drove the overall positive result.[\[1\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Prokopidis K, et al. “Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance.” (details summarized in review and meta‑analysis)")
- The <strong>largest individual trial</strong> in generally healthy young adults (Sandkühler 2023, 5 g/day for 6 weeks, n=123) found <strong>no significant change in cognitive performance or prefrontal activation</strong>.[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- A UK government scientific opinion summarised an RCT in <strong>30 healthy young adults given 10–20 g/day</strong>, which <strong>reported no difference between creatine and placebo for any cognitive measure at any time point</strong>.[\[6\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function> "UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee. UKNHCC scientific opinion: creatine supplementation and improved cognitive function. August 2024")

For <strong>middle‑aged adults</strong>, the evidence is thinner but somewhat more encouraging:

- Chun et al. 2026 studied <strong>45–65‑year‑old sedentary adults</strong> given <strong>10 g/day creatine for 12 weeks, without exercise</strong>. There was a <strong>statistically significant improvement in delayed recall at 6 weeks</strong>, but <strong>no consistent effects across other cognitive domains</strong>, and many analyses were exploratory.[\[16\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13463452/> "Chun Y, et al. Creatine supplementation and cognitive function in middle‑aged sedentary adults: a randomized controlled trial")
- Another small high‑dose (20 g/day) pilot trial in adults found an <strong>≈11% increase in brain total creatine over 8 weeks</strong> with some associated cognitive improvements, but sample size (n≈20) and multiplicity issues limit interpretability.[\[17\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Forbes SC, et al. Creatine supplementation increases brain creatine and may influence cognition: a pilot trial")

McMorris’ 2024 review, synthesizing these and similar trials, concluded that <strong>creatine does not reliably improve cognitive function in healthy young adults</strong>, in contrast to popular claims.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review")

<strong>Relevance for you:</strong> As a cognitively intact 38‑year‑old, the <em>best</em> RCT evidence says <strong>do not expect a noticeable chronic cognitive boost</strong> from creatine, even though pooled analyses detect small average effects.

## 4 3. Creatine Under Sleep Deprivation and Cognitive Stress

<strong>Key insight:</strong> Here the signal is stronger and more relevant to your intermittent sleep loss. The open question is whether the <strong>acute “rescue” effect under severe deprivation</strong> matters for your real‑world pattern of somewhat short nights.

### 3.1 Acute high‑dose trials in sleep‑deprived adults

Several RCTs have now tested creatine under experimentally induced sleep deprivation.

- <strong>McMorris 2006</strong>: After 24 h of sleep deprivation, a creatine loading protocol <strong>improved mood state and performance on tasks placing heavy demands on the prefrontal cortex</strong>.[\[18\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "McMorris T, et al. Creatine supplementation, sleep deprivation, and cognitive performance")
- <strong>Gordji‑Nejad et al. 2024 (Scientific Reports)</strong>: In young adults subjected to ≈21 h of sleep deprivation, a <strong>single large dose of creatine (\~0.35 g/kg)</strong> improved cognitive performance (e.g., reaction time and accuracy) and altered cerebral high‑energy phosphate levels.[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation") Reported improvements were on the order of <strong>10–25% vs sleep‑deprived baseline</strong> over several hours.
- <strong>Gordji‑Nejad et al. 2026 (Nutrients)</strong>: A <strong>single 0.2 g/kg dose</strong> again <strong>reduced deterioration in cognitive performance during sleep loss</strong>, with about \~12% better preservation of performance compared with placebo.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")

These effects align tightly with the mechanistic story: sleep deprivation lowers brain energy availability and increases prefrontal metabolic stress; creatine buffers ATP, so tasks heavily taxing the prefrontal cortex benefit the most.

### 3.2 Contrasting evidence from loading protocols

A much smaller 2026 pilot trial (n=8) using a <strong>7‑day 20 g/day loading protocol</strong> failed to show clear benefits under sleep restriction, in contrast with the single‑dose studies.[\[19\]](<https://link.springer.com/> "Shaw G, et al. Creatine loading and cognitive performance during sleep restriction: a pilot trial") Methodological issues (tiny sample, different timing, less severe deprivation) make it hard to interpret, but it underscores that <strong>protocol choice matters</strong>.

Overall, the <strong>weight of evidence supports an acute benefit of high‑dose creatine during substantial sleep deprivation</strong>, with repeated demonstrations across at least three independent trials.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation")[\[18\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "McMorris T, et al. Creatine supplementation, sleep deprivation, and cognitive performance")

<strong>Relevance for you:</strong>

- If you occasionally have <strong>truly short or missed nights and still must perform demanding cognitive tasks the next day</strong>, a <strong>single \~0.2 g/kg creatine dose that morning</strong> (≈14 g for a 70 kg person) is one of the more plausible, mechanistically grounded interventions to <em>reduce performance decline</em>, not to exceed your rested baseline.
- For <strong>milder chronic sleep curtailment (e.g., 6–7 h instead of 8 h)</strong>, there is <strong>almost no direct evidence</strong>. The trials use extreme deprivation models (≈21–24 h awake).

## 5 4. How Do Regulatory Decisions and Meta‑Analyses Diverge?

<strong>Key insight:</strong> Regulatory bodies require very high standards of consistency, clinical relevance, and methodological rigor. Meta‑analyses can detect small significant effects that still fail these thresholds.

### 4.1 EFSA and UKNHCC opinions (2024)

In 2024, both EFSA and the UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee evaluated applications to allow claims that <strong>“daily creatine supplementation can contribute to improved cognitive function.”</strong>

- The UK opinion reviewed RCTs in healthy young adults and concluded one key study <strong>“reported no difference between creatine supplementation and placebo groups for any cognitive outcome, at any time‑point”</strong>.[\[6\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function> "UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee. UKNHCC scientific opinion: creatine supplementation and improved cognitive function. August 2024")
- EFSA’s opinion ultimately <strong>rejected the creatine–cognition health claim</strong>, stating that <strong>“the health claims linking creatine and cognitive function improvement are not scientifically supported”</strong> at the level required for an approved EU claim.[\[7\]](<https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2024/12/12/stakeholders-react-to-efsas-health-claim-rejection-of-creatine-for-cognitive-function/> "Stakeholders react to EFSA’s health claim rejection of creatine for cognitive function")

These bodies put particular weight on:

- <strong>Largest, best‑controlled RCTs</strong>,
- <strong>Population‑relevant endpoints</strong> (everyday cognition, not marginal test score shifts),
- Risk of bias, multiplicity, and publication bias.

Their conclusion does not mean <strong>no effect exists</strong>; it means <strong>the evidence does not reach the bar of consistent, clinically meaningful, generalizable benefit</strong> for the <em>general adult population</em>.

### 4.2 Reconciling with positive meta‑analyses

Meta‑analyses like Xu 2024 and Prokopidis 2023 do show <strong>statistically significant improvements in memory and processing speed</strong> with creatine.[\[1\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Prokopidis K, et al. “Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance.” (details summarized in review and meta‑analysis)")[\[2\]](<https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full> "Xu T, et al. Creatine monohydrate supplementation on cognitive function in adults: A systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized controlled trials") But:

- <strong>Effect sizes are small</strong> (memory SMD ≈0.3),
- <strong>Heterogeneity is substantial</strong>, and
- A small number of positive small trials (e.g., Rae 2003) carry disproportionate weight relative to larger null trials (e.g., Sandkühler 2023).[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")

A 2026 commentary explicitly raised <strong>methodological concerns with unit‑of‑analysis errors</strong> in at least one of these meta‑analyses, cautioning against over‑interpretation.[\[20\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Commentary on Xu et al. creatine–cognition meta‑analysis: methodological concerns")

<strong>Implication for you:</strong> Headlines like “Creatine boosts memory” usually reflect <strong>statistically significant but small effects</strong> concentrated in certain subgroups, not a regulatory‑grade demonstration of meaningful improvement in the general population.

## 6 5. Effect Size and Everyday Significance

<strong>Key insight:</strong> Even where creatine helps, the average gains are <strong>small enough that many individuals won’t notice them in daily life</strong>, though they could matter at the margins for very demanding or stressed situations.

### 5.1 Translating SMD ≈ 0.3 into real‑world terms

An SMD (Cohen’s <em>d</em>) of \~0.3 is typically labelled a <strong>“small” effect</strong>. In cognitive testing, this can correspond roughly to:

- <strong>A couple of points on an IQ‑type scale</strong>, or
- <strong>A fraction of a standard deviation in working memory tasks</strong>, e.g., perhaps <strong>a fraction of a digit in backward digit span</strong>, depending on the exact test and baseline variance.

Within Rae 2003, the <em>within‑trial</em> effect on backward digit span was quite noticeable (going from ≈7.0 to 8.5 items on average).[\[11\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13005770/> "Rae C, Digney AL, McEwan SR, Bates TC. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves memory and intelligence in vegetarian young adults") But that is <strong>one of the most extreme, and in hindsight probably optimistic, single‑study results</strong>; subsequent larger trials did not replicate such large improvements.[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")

In real‑world desk work:

- <strong>Sustained attention and working memory</strong> do matter—small increments could translate to slightly fewer mistakes when very tired or under acute demand.
- At the same time, <strong>sleep duration, circadian alignment, caffeine use, physical activity, and workload management</strong> almost certainly dwarf the effect of creatine.

### 5.2 Under sleep deprivation

In contrast, the <strong>≈10–25% performance preservation</strong> seen in acute sleep‑deprivation trials is large enough to be <strong>behaviorally obvious</strong>, but only <strong>relative to a strongly impaired state</strong>.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation") Creatine is not making subjects better than well‑rested controls; it is <strong>mitigating the drop</strong>.

<strong>Implication for you:</strong> Chronic daily creatine is very unlikely to make you <em>feel</em> sharper in ordinary well‑rested days. During a severe all‑nighter, an acute high dose might make the difference between <strong>serious cognitive slowing and merely “a bit off”</strong>—but that is a rare, high‑stress context.

## 7 6. Doses, Forms, and Safety

### 6.1 Doses used in cognition studies

Cognitive RCTs have used a range of doses:

- <strong>Chronic “sports‑style” protocols</strong>

  - <strong>Loading</strong>: 20 g/day for 5–7 days (split into 4 doses), followed by
  - <strong>Maintenance</strong>: 3–5 g/day.
- <strong>Vegetarian/young adult cognition trials</strong>

  - Rae 2003: 5 g/day for 6 weeks.[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")
  - Benton 2011: 20 g/day for 5 days.[\[12\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21118604/> "Benton D, Donohoe RT. The influence of creatine supplementation on the cognitive functioning of vegetarians and omnivores")
  - Sandkühler 2023: 5 g/day for 6 weeks.[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- <strong>Older/middle‑aged adult trials</strong>

  - Chun 2026: 10 g/day for 12 weeks.[\[16\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13463452/> "Chun Y, et al. Creatine supplementation and cognitive function in middle‑aged sedentary adults: a randomized controlled trial")
  - Other aging cognition studies typically use 5–20 g/day, sometimes combined with resistance training.[\[9\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40971619/> "Marshall AC, et al. Creatine and cognition in aging: a systematic review")
- <strong>Sleep deprivation experiments</strong>

  - Single <strong>0.2–0.35 g/kg</strong> doses (≈14–25 g for a 70 kg person).[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation")[\[8\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11820685/> "Pratt J, et al. Creatine for brain health and cognition: current evidence and perspectives")

A 2026 review suggested that <strong>raising brain creatine probably requires sustained higher daily doses (around 5–10 g/day) for weeks</strong>, with brain tCr increases in the <strong>3–10%</strong> range.[\[21\]](<https://theconversation.com/> "The Conversation. “How much creatine do you need for brain health?”")[\[22\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Brain creatine changes with supplementation in humans")

The <strong>standard, practical dose for long‑term use is 3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate</strong>, which is what most sports and safety literature is built around.

### 6.2 Safety profile

On safety, the evidence is unusually strong:

- A 2025 pooled safety analysis of <strong>685 human clinical trials</strong> found <strong>no significant difference in the prevalence or frequency of side effects between creatine and placebo</strong> across <strong>49 different outcomes</strong>, including kidney and liver markers.[\[23\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11983583/> "Kreider RB, et al. Safety of creatine supplementation: analysis of the frequency of reported side effects in clinical trials")[\[24\]](<https://www.supplysidesj.com/healthy-living/analysis-finds-no-support-for-concerns-about-creatine-side-effects> "Kreider RB. Analysis finds no support for concerns about creatine side effects")
- The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand and subsequent reviews state that even <strong>up to 30 g/day for 5 years</strong> has not been associated with clinically relevant renal, hepatic, or cardiovascular harm in healthy individuals.[\[25\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3407788/> "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: creatine supplementation and safety")
- Mild issues (when they occur) are usually <strong>water retention, minor weight gain, and transient gastrointestinal upset</strong>, especially if large doses are taken all at once without food.[\[23\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11983583/> "Kreider RB, et al. Safety of creatine supplementation: analysis of the frequency of reported side effects in clinical trials")[\[24\]](<https://www.supplysidesj.com/healthy-living/analysis-finds-no-support-for-concerns-about-creatine-side-effects> "Kreider RB. Analysis finds no support for concerns about creatine side effects")

These data reflect <strong>primarily physically active and clinical populations</strong>, but the mechanisms (osmotic water shifts and benign creatinine elevation) are not specific to athletes.

<strong>Caveats:</strong>

- People with <strong>pre‑existing kidney disease</strong> or on nephrotoxic medications should avoid creatine or use only under medical supervision (not because trials show harm, but because safety data in this specific group are limited and creatinine lab values become harder to interpret).
- Creatine slightly <strong>raises serum creatinine</strong>, which can confound estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculations, although this is a measurement artifact rather than true dysfunction.

<strong>Implication for you:</strong> At 3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate, <strong>safety is not the limiting factor</strong>; the question is entirely whether the modest, uncertain cognitive benefit is worth the (low) hassle and cost.

## 8 7. Scenario Analysis: When Creatine for Cognition Makes More vs Less Sense

### Scenario A: You want broad, everyday cognitive enhancement while otherwise healthy and rested

- <strong>Evidence:</strong> RCTs in healthy young/middle‑aged adults mostly show <strong>null or very small effects</strong>; regulators rejected general cognition claims.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review")[\[6\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function> "UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee. UKNHCC scientific opinion: creatine supplementation and improved cognitive function. August 2024")[\[7\]](<https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2024/12/12/stakeholders-react-to-efsas-health-claim-rejection-of-creatine-for-cognitive-function/> "Stakeholders react to EFSA’s health claim rejection of creatine for cognitive function")[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- <strong>Assessment:</strong> Creatine is <strong>not a reliable “nootropic”</strong> in this context. Lifestyle levers will dominate.

### Scenario B: You are vegetarian, work cognitively hard, and often sleep poorly (your actual case)

- <strong>Evidence:</strong>

  - Vegetarian trials are <strong>mixed</strong>: early strong positives vs later larger neutral results; brain creatine may not be lower than in omnivores.[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")[\[12\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21118604/> "Benton D, Donohoe RT. The influence of creatine supplementation on the cognitive functioning of vegetarians and omnivores")[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")[\[14\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets")[\[15\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine supplementation and brain phosphocreatine in vegetarians and omnivores")
  - Sleep‑deprivation trials show <strong>clear mitigation of acute performance decline</strong> with large single doses.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation")[\[18\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "McMorris T, et al. Creatine supplementation, sleep deprivation, and cognitive performance")
  - Meta‑analyses suggest <strong>small average memory and speed gains</strong>, especially with lower baseline creatine or under stress.[\[1\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Prokopidis K, et al. “Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance.” (details summarized in review and meta‑analysis)")[\[2\]](<https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1424972/full> "Xu T, et al. Creatine monohydrate supplementation on cognitive function in adults: A systematic review and meta‑analysis of randomized controlled trials")[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[8\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11820685/> "Pratt J, et al. Creatine for brain health and cognition: current evidence and perspectives")
- <strong>Assessment:</strong>

  - <strong>Chronic low‑dose creatine (3–5 g/day)</strong>: plausible small cognitive upside; benefits might only be detectable on heavy, tired days, and even then may be subtle.
  - <strong>Targeted acute high‑dose use on severely sleep‑deprived days</strong>: better supported and more likely to be subjectively noticeable.

### Scenario C: You are older (&gt;60) or moving into that age group and worried about cognitive aging

- <strong>Evidence:</strong> Most of the <strong>positive aging data</strong> and the largest age‑stratified effects come from older adults, sometimes combined with exercise.[\[1\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Prokopidis K, et al. “Creatine supplementation and cognitive performance.” (details summarized in review and meta‑analysis)")[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[9\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40971619/> "Marshall AC, et al. Creatine and cognition in aging: a systematic review")
- <strong>Assessment:</strong> For late middle‑age and beyond, creatine looks <strong>increasingly rational as a multimodal intervention</strong> (strength, bone, possibly cognition), particularly against a vegetarian, low‑creatine dietary background.

## 9 8. Practical Recommendations

Prioritized from immediate/low‑cost to more involved.

### 8.1 Immediate: Clarify your objectives and baseline

1. <strong>Clarify what you actually want from creatine.</strong>

    

   - If the goal is <strong>general sharpness</strong>: expectations should be low.
   - If the goal is <strong>resilience on rare, very short‑sleep days</strong>: evidence is stronger.
2. <strong>If you decide to trial creatine, treat it as an experiment.</strong>

    

   - Before starting, note subjective issues you care about (afternoon fatigue, error rate under time pressure, how you feel after short nights).
   - Track them over 4–8 weeks on creatine; if you don’t notice anything, you can reasonably stop.

### 8.2 Short‑term: If you choose to supplement chronically

<strong>Protocol suggestion (for a healthy 38‑year‑old vegetarian with normal kidney function):</strong>

- <strong>Form:</strong> Creatine monohydrate powder (most studied, cheapest, highly bioavailable).
- <strong>Dose:</strong>

  - Skip loading; <strong>3–5 g once daily with a meal</strong> is adequate to saturate stores over several weeks.
  - If you want to mirror vegetarian cognition trials more closely, <strong>5 g/day</strong> is the standard dose.[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- <strong>Duration for evaluation:</strong> At least <strong>6 weeks</strong>, since some brain creatine and cognitive changes in RCTs emerged after several weeks.[\[10\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14561278/> "Rae C, et al. Oral creatine monohydrate supplementation improves brain performance: a double‑blind, placebo‑controlled, cross‑over trial")[\[16\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13463452/> "Chun Y, et al. Creatine supplementation and cognitive function in middle‑aged sedentary adults: a randomized controlled trial")
- <strong>Monitoring:</strong>

  - If convenient, get <strong>baseline and follow‑up creatinine/eGFR</strong> simply to avoid confusion if your doctor later sees a slightly higher creatinine.
  - Watch for <strong>GI upset or uncomfortable water retention</strong>; splitting the dose or taking with food usually mitigates this.

<strong>Decision rule:</strong>

- If after \~6–8 weeks you <strong>perceive no benefit</strong> and the only clear change is a bit more water weight, <strong>discontinuation is rational</strong>, as current evidence does not justify perseverance for cognition alone.
- If you also value <strong>muscle, strength, or general plant‑based diet support</strong>, continuing at 3–5 g/day remains reasonable even in the absence of a noticeable cognitive change.[\[26\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets: addressing nutritional gaps")[\[27\]](<https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/1/95> "Gutiérrez‑Hellín J, et al. Creatine supplementation in women, vegans, and vegetarians: an MDPI review")

### 8.3 Short‑term: Targeted use for acute sleep loss

For rare <strong>all‑nighters or &lt;3–4 h nights before a demanding day</strong>:

- <strong>Single‑dose protocol:</strong>

   

  - Upon waking (or early in the day), take <strong>≈0.2 g/kg creatine monohydrate</strong> with food and plenty of water (e.g., ≈14 g for 70 kg).[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation")
  - Avoid stacking with large caffeine doses until you know how you respond; both can alter GI comfort and hydration.
- <strong>Caveats:</strong>

   

  - Do not repeat very high doses <strong>daily</strong>; the trials used <strong>single or very occasional acute doses</strong>.
  - This strategy mitigates performance decline; it <strong>does not replace sleep</strong> or eliminate safety risks of sleep deprivation (e.g., driving).

### 8.4 Long‑term: Integrate with broader cognitive health strategy

Given the small effect sizes, creatine should be seen as <strong>peripheral, not central</strong>, to cognitive optimization:

- <strong>Prioritize sleep quantity and regularity.</strong> The effect of going from 6 h to 7.5–8 h sleep will <strong>overwhelm</strong> any plausible creatine effect.
- Maintain or increase <strong>aerobic and resistance exercise</strong>, both independently linked to improved cognition and mood.
- As a vegetarian, ensure adequacy of <strong>B12, iron, omega‑3 (EPA/DHA), and overall protein</strong>; deficiencies here have far larger cognitive consequences than marginal ergogenic supplementation.[\[26\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets: addressing nutritional gaps")[\[27\]](<https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/17/1/95> "Gutiérrez‑Hellín J, et al. Creatine supplementation in women, vegans, and vegetarians: an MDPI review")

## 10 9. Remaining Uncertainties and Research Directions

Several important questions remain unresolved, many of which directly affect someone like you:

- <strong>Who exactly benefits most?</strong> Current data suggest <strong>older adults, acutely sleep‑deprived people, perhaps those with certain neurological conditions</strong> benefit more, but vegetarian status as a moderator is still unclear and may be smaller than initially thought.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review")[\[9\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40971619/> "Marshall AC, et al. Creatine and cognition in aging: a systematic review")[\[14\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine and plant‑based diets")[\[15\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7246861/> "Kaviani M, et al. Creatine supplementation and brain phosphocreatine in vegetarians and omnivores")
- <strong>What brain concentrations are achievable and required for cognitive gains?</strong> Small pilots show <strong>3–10% increases in brain creatine</strong> after high‑dose supplementation,[\[17\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Forbes SC, et al. Creatine supplementation increases brain creatine and may influence cognition: a pilot trial")[\[22\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Brain creatine changes with supplementation in humans") but dose–response curves vs cognitive outcomes are not well characterized.
- <strong>Long‑term cognitive aging effects in mid‑life vegetarians</strong>: there are <strong>no large, long‑duration RCTs starting in the 30s–40s</strong> that track cognitive trajectories with creatine, despite plausible mechanisms.
- <strong>Interaction with sleep quantity and circadian timing</strong>: we have data for <strong>24‑h total deprivation</strong>, but little for chronic partial sleep restriction, which is what most professionals actually experience.

From a research design standpoint, what is needed to definitively answer your question would be a <strong>large, multi‑year RCT</strong> in <strong>middle‑aged adults (including a vegetarian subgroup)</strong>, randomised to 5 g/day creatine vs placebo, with <strong>well‑powered cognitive, sleep, and functional outcomes</strong>. No such study yet exists.

## 11 10. Integrated Answer to Your Original Question

- <strong>Is there a convincing case for creatine <em>purely</em> for cognitive benefit in someone like you?</strong><br>
   No. There is <strong>moderately good evidence of small, domain‑specific benefits (memory, processing speed)</strong> in adults overall, but the <strong>largest and best trials in young/middle‑aged healthy adults are largely negative</strong>, and regulators have rejected generic cognition claims.
- <strong>Do results from older adults, sleep‑deprived participants, or low‑creatine individuals apply to you?</strong><br>
   Only <strong>partially</strong>. As a vegetarian who is sometimes sleep‑deprived, you resemble the <strong>“stressed/low‑baseline” profiles</strong> where creatine is more promising. Yet <strong>vegetarian‑specific RCTs are mixed</strong>, newer data challenge the idea of lower brain creatine in vegetarians, and your age group has not shown robust chronic benefits.
- <strong>How do RCTs compare with headlines and meta‑analyses?</strong><br>
   Headlines oversell. Meta‑analyses are directionally positive but correspond to <strong>small effect sizes</strong> and rely partly on older, smaller positive studies. The <strong>most rigorous recent RCTs and systematic reviews are more skeptical</strong>, describing effects as modest and inconsistent.[\[3\]](<https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0166432824001384> "McMorris T. Creatine supplementation and cognition: A systematic review")[\[6\]](<https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function/uknhcc-scientific-opinion-creatine-supplementation-and-improved-cognitive-function> "UK Nutrition and Health Claims Committee. UKNHCC scientific opinion: creatine supplementation and improved cognitive function. August 2024")[\[7\]](<https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2024/12/12/stakeholders-react-to-efsas-health-claim-rejection-of-creatine-for-cognitive-function/> "Stakeholders react to EFSA’s health claim rejection of creatine for cognitive function")[\[13\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11275561/> "Sandkühler J, et al. Effect of creatine supplementation on cognitive performance in young adults: a randomized, double‑blind, placebo‑controlled crossover trial")
- <strong>Are improvements large enough to matter in everyday life?</strong><br>
   For a cognitively healthy 38‑year‑old, <strong>probably not in any dramatic way</strong>. You might get a <strong>subtle edge on demanding, tired days</strong>, and <strong>a more tangible benefit during severe sleep deprivation with a single high dose</strong>, but not a transformation in baseline performance.
- <strong>What about doses and safety?</strong><br>
   <strong>3–5 g/day creatine monohydrate</strong> is the standard, with <strong>excellent safety data</strong> over years of use.\[23–25\] Cognitive trials have used up to 20 g/day chronically and 0.2–0.35 g/kg acutely without serious adverse events in healthy adults, though such high doses are unnecessary outside experimental or acute sleep‑loss contexts.[\[4\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12832544/> "Li R, et al. Creatine and brain health: A narrative review")[\[5\]](<https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-024-54249-9> "Gordji‑Nejad A, et al. Single-dose creatine improves cognitive performance and cerebral high‑energy phosphates during sleep deprivation")[\[16\]](<https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13463452/> "Chun Y, et al. Creatine supplementation and cognitive function in middle‑aged sedentary adults: a randomized controlled trial")[\[17\]](<https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/> "Forbes SC, et al. Creatine supplementation increases brain creatine and may influence cognition: a pilot trial")

On balance, for you creatine is best viewed as a <strong>safe, low‑cost, low‑yield “option value” supplement</strong>: worth considering if you also value its muscular and general vegetarian‑nutrition benefits, possibly useful as an acute tool after an all‑nighter, but <strong>not a core pillar of cognitive performance strategy.</strong>
