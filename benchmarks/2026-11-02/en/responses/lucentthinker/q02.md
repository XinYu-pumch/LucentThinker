# Bottom line

**No — the human evidence does not make a convincing case that creatine will improve your memory or mental performance in everyday life.** The largest and best-matched randomized trial found at most a very small working-memory effect, no effect on reasoning or eight other cognitive tasks, and no greater benefit in vegetarians [162]. The meta-analyses behind the headlines report small memory effects, but the most-cited one is statistically compromised and the other lost its overall significance after correction [1][62]. EU and UK regulators have declined to accept a creatine–cognition health claim [143][145][154], and a 2026 review concluded that creatine should be regarded “not as a proven cognitive enhancer, but as a physiologically compelling hypothesis still awaiting definitive clinical validation” [129].

Creatine remains a reasonable, cheap and generally safe supplement for strength and muscle [351]. The brain story is interesting, but it is not yet a reason for a healthy 38-year-old to expect a sharper mind.

## 1. What the best randomized trials actually show

The most informative trial is Sandkühler et al. (2023): **123 adults, mean age 31, about half vegetarian**, randomized to **5 g/day creatine monohydrate or placebo for 6 weeks each** in a preregistered, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover [162]. It was powered to detect a moderate effect (Cohen’s d = 0.45) [162].

- **Working memory (Backward Digit Span):** p = 0.064, η² = 0.029. The raw difference was 0.41 correct items — about a **0.2-digit longer span** — with Cohen’s d = 0.17, roughly 2.5 IQ points if converted [162].
- **Reasoning (Raven’s Advanced Progressive Matrices):** p = 0.327, d = 0.09; 0.23 more items, roughly **1 IQ point** [162].
- **Eight exploratory tasks** covering attention, task-switching, verbal learning, visuospatial memory, Stroop and verbal fluency: no indication of benefit [162].
- **Vegetarians did not benefit more than omnivores**; the nonsignificant creatine effect was, if anything, smaller in vegetarians [162].
- **Side effects** were reported more often on creatine (RR 4.25), although no one discontinued [162].

Two other trials point the same way. In **30 healthy young adults**, **10 or 20 g/day for 6 weeks** did not improve processing speed, episodic memory or attention, or change prefrontal activation [371]. In **older women**, **20 g/day for 5 days followed by 5 g/day for 24 weeks** produced no cognitive benefit [199].

These trials used laboratory cognitive tests. None measured real-world work output — spreadsheet accuracy, error rates, meeting performance or deadline mistakes — so even the positive signals are about test scores, not your desk job [162][13].

## 2. Why the meta-analyses and headlines diverge

### Xu et al. (2024)

This meta-analysis pooled **16 RCTs and 492 participants**, aged 20.8–76.4 years, mostly using creatine monohydrate [13][5]. It reported:

- **Memory:** SMD 0.31 (95% CI 0.18–0.44), with moderate certainty [13].
- **Overall cognitive function and executive function:** no significant improvement [13].
- **Attention and processing speed:** the abstract reports significant effects, but the paper’s own results sections — quoted in a 2025 corrigendum — report null estimates for those domains (attention SMD 0.22, 95% CI −0.40 to 0.84; processing speed SMD 0.01, 95% CI −0.38 to 0.40) [13][116]. That internal inconsistency is itself a warning about the summary claims.

More fundamentally, several included trials contributed multiple correlated cognitive outcomes from the same participants, which were treated as independent effect sizes. This “double-counting” inflates the apparent sample size and precision [1]. EFSA reviewed the same meta-analysis and concluded that **“no conclusions can be drawn”** from it for substantiating a health claim [144].

### Prokopidis et al. (2023)

This meta-analysis pooled **8 RCTs** and found an overall memory effect of **SMD 0.29 (95% CI 0.04–0.53)** [62]. The age split was striking:

- **Older adults, 66–76 years:** SMD 0.88 (95% CI 0.22–1.55; I² = 83%) [19][62].
- **Younger adults, 11–31 years:** SMD 0.03 (95% CI −0.14 to 0.20) — essentially zero [19].

But **six of the eight trials were rated high risk of bias**, largely because randomization was inadequately reported, and publication bias could not be assessed [62]. After the double-counting correction, the overall memory effect was no longer significant, while the older-adult signal persisted [1]. Secondary summaries of the authors’ reply put the corrected overall effect at **SMD ≈0.19 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.46)** and the older-adult effect at **SMD ≈0.80 (95% CI 0.25–1.34)** [429][430]. I could not retrieve the full text of the authors’ reply itself, so those exact figures should be treated as secondary [438].

There is also a direct contradiction between the two meta-analyses: Xu et al. reported greater benefit in adults aged **18–60**, whereas Prokopidis et al. found the benefit concentrated in adults aged **66–76** [13][62]. When two meta-analyses of largely overlapping small trials disagree about who benefits, the honest conclusion is that the subgroup signal is unstable.

### The headlines

A representative article reported **“14% better recall and learning,” “19% faster thinking,” and “13% longer focus”** from the 16-trial meta-analysis [8]. Those are headline conversions of standardized effect sizes, not raw improvements in test scores [8][13]. In the largest trial, the raw differences were about **0.2 of a digit and 1 IQ point** [162]. A 14–19% improvement in memory is not what those data show.

## 3. Do the older-adult, sleep-deprivation and vegetarian findings apply to you?

### Older adults: a real signal, but low-quality and not your situation

The 2026 systematic review by Marshall et al. included only **six studies and 1,542 participants aged 55+**. Only **two were double-blind intervention studies**; four were cross-sectional dietary surveys. Five of six reported a positive association, but the quality ratings were **one “good,” two “fair,” and three “poor,”** and the authors concluded that the evidence is “limited” and that high-quality trials are needed [86]. A 2026 letter in the same journal called the older-adult evidence **“promising but unproven”** [331].

The best long-term RCT in older women was null [199]. So even the older-adult signal is fragile, and it is a signal about age-related vulnerability — not evidence that a healthy 38-year-old will think better.

### Sleep deprivation: large effects only under extreme, artificial conditions

The most dramatic results come from acute total sleep deprivation. In a **15-person crossover trial**, a single **0.35 g/kg** dose of creatine (about **25 g for a 70 kg adult**) during **21 hours of sleep deprivation** improved:

- Word-memory accuracy by **10.3%**;
- Word-memory processing speed by **17.7%**;
- Language processing speed by **29.1%**;
- Logic processing speed by **16.0%**;
- Numeric processing speed by **24.0%** [193].

A 2026 trial in **29 adults** (including 9 vegetarians) using **0.2 g/kg** (about **14 g for 70 kg**) during 21 hours of sleep deprivation reported smaller improvements, **up to about 12%** on some tasks, and concluded the effect was less pronounced than with 0.35 g/kg [31].

But these are not studies of ordinary short sleep. They are single-dose studies in 15–29 people, mostly young, during 21–36 hours of total sleep deprivation [193][31][144]. The study closest to real life — **20 g/day for 7 days followed by a 3-hour sleep restriction night** — found **no effect on any cognitive or physiological variable** in 8 completers, although it was underpowered to exclude small effects [21]. Older studies found effects mainly on complex executive tasks during 24–36 hours of deprivation, with methodological limitations [144].

So: promising for acute, severe sleep loss at high single doses; not proven for a few short nights or for standard 5 g/day. It is not a substitute for sleep.

### Vegetarians and low dietary creatine: plausible for muscle, not established for brain

Vegetarians do have lower dietary creatine intake and lower muscle creatine. But the brain is not muscle. In the one direct MRS comparison, vegetarians and omnivores had **comparable total creatine in the posterior cingulate cortex** — 5.999 vs 5.917 IU, p = 0.77 — despite dietary intakes of **0.03 vs 1.34 g/day** [380].

The largest direct test found **no greater cognitive benefit in vegetarians** [162]. The older positive trials were small and short:

- **Benton & Donohoe (2010):** 128 young women, 20 g/day for 5 days; memory was better in vegetarians, but verbal fluency and vigilance were unaffected [40]. EFSA re-examined the study and concluded it did not show an effect of creatine on any cognitive domain when properly analyzed [144].
- **Rae et al. (2003):** 45 young vegetarians, 5 g/day for 6 weeks; reported p < 0.0001 for working memory and reasoning [54]. But EFSA found methodological limitations, and the large replication found strong evidence against Rae’s effect size being representative [144][162].

Being vegetarian is not a reason to expect a cognitive benefit.

## 4. Are the improvements large enough to matter in everyday life?

No. The meta-analytic memory effects are **SMD ≈0.2–0.3**, conventionally “small” [13][62]. In the largest trial, the raw differences were **about 0.2 of a digit span and about 1 IQ point** [162]. Effects of that size are below what you would notice in a demanding desk job, and they are smaller than the effects of sleep, practice or engagement with the task.

The only large effects are in acute, severe sleep deprivation at **0.2–0.35 g/kg single doses** — a different physiological situation, with much less evidence and much higher doses [193][31]. No trial has tested whether creatine improves real work productivity, error rates or learning in a desk-job setting [162][13].

## 5. Doses studied

| Protocol | Participants | Cognitive result |
|---|---|---|
| **5 g/day × 6 weeks** | 123 adults, half vegetarian [162] | Tiny/null: ~0.2 digit, d = 0.17; reasoning and exploratory tasks null |
| **10 or 20 g/day × 6 weeks** | 30 healthy young adults [371] | Null for processing speed, episodic memory, attention |
| **20 g/day × 5 days** | 128 young women [40] | Memory better in vegetarians only; EFSA negative [144] |
| **20 g/day × 5 days, then 5 g/day × 24 weeks** | Older women [199] | Null |
| **0.2 g/kg single dose** (~14 g/70 kg) | 29 adults, 21 h sleep deprivation [31] | Up to ~12% improvement on some tasks |
| **0.35 g/kg single dose** (~25 g/70 kg) | 15 adults, 21 h sleep deprivation [193] | ~10–29% improvement on selected tasks |
| **20 g/day × 7 days** | 8 adults, 3 h sleep restriction [21] | Null |
| **20 g/day × 4 weeks** | 6 adults, brain MRS [223] | +8.7% brain creatine; cognition not tested |

The standard protocol is **20 g/day (about 0.3 g/kg/day) for 5–7 days**, followed by **3–5 g/day**, or **3 g/day for 28 days** [351]. Cognitive trials have used **2.2–20 g/day for 5 days to 24 weeks** [62].

There is an important dose–target mismatch. For the brain, a 2026 review concluded that increases are most likely with **20 g/day or 0.3 g/kg/day for at least 4 weeks**, while 5–7-day protocols largely failed; it did not establish that 5 g/day is sufficient [223]. Only **2 of 9** healthy-cohort studies showed clear brain-creatine increases, and MRS measurement error can be up to 20% [223]. So the dose best established for muscle may not be the dose that reaches the brain, and the dose that reaches the brain has the least cognitive-outcome evidence.

## 6. Safety

Creatine monohydrate is generally safe in healthy people with normal kidney function.

- The **2017 ISSN position stand** concludes that short- and long-term use **up to 30 g/day for 5 years** is safe and well tolerated, with **no compelling evidence of renal dysfunction**; the only consistently reported side effect is weight gain, largely from **0.5–1.0 L of fluid retention** [351]. People with pre-existing kidney disease are advised to consult a physician [351].
- A **2025 analysis of 685 trials** (12,839 creatine participants vs 13,452 placebo) found side-effect reporting rates of **13.7% vs 13.2%** (p = 0.776), with small absolute increases in gastrointestinal symptoms (**4.9% vs 4.3%**) and muscle cramping (**2.9% vs 0.9%**) [401].
- A **2026 dose–response review** of 684 RCTs and more than 12,800 participants found no consistent dose- or duration-dependent increase in side effects; reported symptoms were mainly mild and nonspecific [405].
- In the largest cognitive trial, more participants reported negative side effects on creatine (RR 4.25), but no one discontinued [162].

The kidney issue is mostly a measurement artifact. Creatine is metabolized to creatinine, so serum creatinine rises and creatinine-based eGFR can fall without kidney injury. A 2026 meta-analysis of 26 RCTs found a serum-creatinine increase of **0.14 mg/dL** and a creatinine-based eGFR decrease of **10.75 mL/min**, but measured GFR by Cr-EDTA was **not significantly different (+5.89 mL/min; p = 0.06)**, and the authors concluded there was no evidence of kidney injury [258]. A 2025 meta-analysis similarly found a small creatinine rise with no significant change in GFR [257]. This can confuse routine kidney blood tests, so tell your doctor if you take it.

## 7. Remaining uncertainties

- **Brain creatine response:** only 2 of 9 studies show clear increases; MRS error can be up to 20%; the optimal dose and duration are unknown [223].
- **Meta-analysis fragility:** double counting, high risk of bias, small trials and publication bias that could not be assessed mean the true effect in healthy adults may be close to zero [1][62].
- **Age subgroup inconsistency:** Xu found greater benefit at 18–60; Prokopidis found it at 66–76 [13][62].
- **No long-term cognitive outcomes:** the 2026 critical review concluded that the field still lacks the rigorous, reproducible trials needed to establish cognitive benefit, including in patient populations [129].
- **No real-world work-performance trials:** the evidence is lab-task based [162][13].
- **Responder variability:** baseline creatine, diet, sex, genetics and brain uptake may matter, but we cannot reliably identify who responds [62][129].
- **Regulatory status:** EFSA concluded in 2024 that a cause-and-effect relationship had not been established [143][144]; the EU refused the claim in **Regulation 2026/1118** [145]; the UK committee reached a similar conclusion for **≤3 g/day** [154].

A note on verification: some primary sources were available only as abstracts or partial text — including the EFSA opinion [143][144], the UK opinion [154], the Prokopidis full text [62], the 2026 0.2 g/kg sleep trial [31] and the 2026 brain-creatine review [223]. The corrected Prokopidis estimates come from secondary summaries [429][430] because the full authors’ reply was not retrievable [438]. The direction of the evidence is consistent, but exact figures should be read with that caveat.

## 8. Practical answer for you

- **Do not take creatine expecting a cognitive edge.** The expected effect is tiny or zero, and below what you would notice [162][13].
- **If you want creatine for strength, muscle or general nutrition,** **3–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate** is a reasonable, cheap, safe choice; no loading phase is needed [351].
- **If you are curious about a personal cognitive trial,** 5 g/day for 8–12 weeks is low-risk, but any change will be within noise. Do not use 20–25 g single doses to chase the sleep-deprivation data; those were single-dose laboratory studies in 15–29 people [193][31].
- **Treat sleep as the primary intervention.** The sleep-deprivation studies are about acute total deprivation, not ordinary short nights, and a standard 20 g/day protocol failed in a 3-hour restriction model [21][31].
- **If you have kidney disease or are on medications,** check with a clinician before starting [351].
- **Buy plain creatine monohydrate** from a reputable brand. It is the most studied and effective form; there is no good reason to pay for fancy alternatives [351].

The honest summary: **creatine is a proven ergogenic for muscle, a plausible but unproven cognitive aid, and a poor substitute for sleep** [129][351].
