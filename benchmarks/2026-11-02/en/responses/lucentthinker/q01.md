# Home battery in Bristol: 5 kWh vs 10 kWh vs keeping your current setup

*As of 29 October 2026. All figures are indicative model estimates, not quotes.*

## The short answer

**On the numbers you gave, keeping the current setup is the better financial decision.** Neither battery pays back within its 10-year warranty on a flat 27p/15p tariff.

The 5 kWh and 10 kWh are really two different products for two different jobs:

- **5 kWh is the right size for capturing your solar surplus.** It matches the roughly 2,340 kWh you currently export, but it saves only about £110/year — a payback of roughly 35 years.
- **10 kWh is the right size for covering your evening load and arbitraging a cheap overnight tariff.** On solar alone it saves only about £160/year, because the extra capacity has nothing new to charge from. It only becomes interesting if you can fill it cheaply every night.

| Option | Indicative annual benefit | Simple payback | 10-year net* | What it means |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **Keep current setup** | — | — | — | Best financial case on these numbers |
| 5 kWh, flat 27p/15p | ~£110 | ~35 years | about −£3,000 | Right size for solar self-use; not an investment |
| 10 kWh, flat 27p/15p | ~£160 | ~33 years | about −£4,000 | Extra capacity not justified by solar alone |
| 5 kWh + Octopus Flux | ~£220–240 | 16–18 years | about −£2,000 | Better, still well outside warranty |
| 10 kWh + Octopus Flux | ~£340–490 | 11–16 years | about −£1,900 to −£1,200 | Closest solar/tariff case; still short |
| 5 kWh + ~8p overnight tariff | ~£240–280 | 14–16 years | about −£1,700 | Cheap tariff does not rescue 5 kWh |
| 10 kWh + ~8p overnight tariff | ~£500–650 | 8–11 years | about −£500 to +£100 | Only near-viable case; eligibility is the catch |

\*Undiscounted, constant prices, 85% average output over 10 years, no replacement, standing charges excluded. The calculations are shown below.

The rest of this note explains where those numbers come from, which conclusions are robust, and which depend on your actual half-hourly data.

## 2. What a battery actually delivers

Nameplate capacity is not usable energy. You have to apply depth of discharge and round-trip efficiency:

**Usable AC energy per cycle ≈ nominal capacity × depth of discharge × AC round-trip efficiency**

| Product | Nominal | DoD | Usable DC | Assumed AC round-trip | Usable AC per cycle |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Fox ESS EP5 | 5.18 kWh | 90% | 4.66 kWh | 90% | **~4.2 kWh** |
| Fox ESS EP11 | 10.36 kWh | 90% | 9.32 kWh | 90% | **~8.4 kWh** |
| Alpha ESS Smile G3 10.1 | 10.1 kWh | 95% | 9.60 kWh | 90% | **~8.6 kWh** |
| Sunsynk W10.6 | 10.64 kWh | 90% | 9.58 kWh | 90% | **~8.6 kWh** |
| Tesla Powerwall 3 | 13.5 kWh usable | — | 13.5 kWh | 89% | **~12.0 kWh** |

Fox quotes a **battery-pack** round-trip efficiency of at least 95%, but that is not the full AC-to-AC figure once the inverter, standby losses and wiring are included [116]. A 90% AC-to-AC assumption is a reasonable planning figure; the real number will vary by product and operating conditions. Alpha’s 10.1 kWh module gives about 9.6 kWh usable at 95% DoD [136]. Sunsynk’s W10.6 gives 9.58 kWh usable at 90% DoD [401]. Tesla quotes 89% efficiency via the battery and 13.5 kWh usable [45].

### Degradation and warranty

|---|---|---|---:|---|

Two practical points:

- **A 10-year warranty is not always 10 years of useful cycling.** On throughput-capped products, daily cycling can exhaust the energy allowance before the calendar term. Fox’s EP5 allowance of 16.2 MWh implies roughly 3,100–3,500 full cycles, which is about 9 years at one cycle a day [135]. Alpha’s unlimited-cycle treatment for solar/TOU use is a genuine advantage if you intend to arbitrage every night [136].
- **Degradation reduces the savings each year.** A warranty that guarantees 70% at year 10 implies an average of about 85% output over the period. The 10-year totals below use 8.5 “year-one-equivalent” years of savings, which is why they are lower than ten times the first-year figure.

## 3. Base case: no tariff change, 27p import / 15p export

If the battery only stores surplus solar and discharges it in the evening, the value of each kWh put into the battery is:

**Net value = (round-trip efficiency × import price) − export price**

At 27p import, 15p export and 90% efficiency:

**0.90 × 27p − 15p = 9.3p per kWh stored**

That is the central number. The battery is not saving 27p per kWh; it is saving 24.3p of import but giving up 15p of export, so the net gain is 9.3p. This result follows from the prices and efficiency alone — it does not depend on your load shape.

### The ceiling

Your entire solar surplus is 2,340 kWh/year. Even if a battery could capture **every single kWh** of it — which no battery can, because of capacity and seasonal timing — the maximum saving at 15p export would be:

**2,340 × 9.3p = £218/year**

At an installed cost of £3,950 for 5 kWh, that ceiling gives a best-possible payback of **18 years**. At £5,400 for 10 kWh, it is **25 years**. This is a useful robustness check: no amount of clever battery scheduling can make the solar-only case pay within the warranty at these prices.

### Realistic capture

Using a monthly Bristol solar profile and an evening-heavy load shape consistent with your 35% direct-use figure, the model gives:

| | 5 kWh | 10 kWh |
|---|---:|---:|
| Solar surplus diverted to battery | ~1,190 kWh/yr | ~1,740 kWh/yr |
| Solar delivered to the home | ~1,070 kWh/yr | ~1,560 kWh/yr |
| Share of total surplus captured | ~51% | ~74% |
| Self-consumption rises from 35% to | ~65% | ~78% |
| **Annual saving at 15p export** | **~£110** | **~£160** |
| Installed cost used | £3,950 | £5,400 |
| **Simple payback** | **~35 years** | **~33 years** |
| 10-year saving at 85% average output | ~£935 | ~£1,360 |

The 10 kWh captures about 46% more solar than the 5 kWh, not twice as much. In summer, a 10 kWh battery may fill but the evening load cannot absorb a full 8.4 kWh cycle before midnight; in winter, there is little surplus to fill either battery. The extra 5 kWh therefore has a low marginal value:

**Incremental cost: ~£1,450. Incremental saving at 15p: ~£50/year. Incremental payback: ~29 years.**

That is the key argument against the 10 kWh for pure solar self-consumption. The first 5 kWh captures the best surplus; the second 5 kWh captures the leftovers.

Bristol’s typical yield is around 950–1,050 kWh/kWp, which would put a 4 kWp system at 3,800–4,200 kWh/year [312][303]. Your 3,600 kWh is about 900 kWh/kWp — slightly below typical, possibly due to orientation, shading or a conservative estimate [302][304]. If your real generation is lower than 3,600 kWh, the battery case weakens further.

## 4. The export income you give up

This is the most sensitive assumption in the calculation. The net value of each kWh stored is:

| Export rate | Net value per kWh stored | 5 kWh annual saving | 10 kWh annual saving | 5 kWh payback | 10 kWh payback |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 15p | 9.3p | ~£110 | ~£160 | ~35 yrs | ~33 yrs |
| 12p | 12.3p | ~£145 | ~£215 | ~27 yrs | ~25 yrs |
| 10p | 14.3p | ~£170 | ~£250 | ~23 yrs | ~22 yrs |
| 4.1p | 20.2p | ~£240 | ~£350 | ~16 yrs | ~15 yrs |
| 0p | 24.3p | ~£290 | ~£420 | ~14 yrs | ~13 yrs |

At a high export rate, storing solar is barely worth the round-trip loss. At the 4.1p mandatory SEG rate, the saving roughly doubles — but even then the payback is 15–16 years, still outside the warranty. Only if exported solar were worth nothing at all does the 5 kWh approach a 14-year payback.

This is why your 15p assumption matters so much. If you are actually on a 4–5p export tariff, the battery looks better than the base case; if you are on 15p, it looks worse. And if the 15p is **FIT deemed export**, the position may be better still: Ofgem guidance indicates deemed export can continue where the generation meter measures only the accredited installation, subject to metering and your FIT licensee’s rules [528][281]. In that case you may keep the 15p on generation while also using battery-stored solar to avoid imports, which effectively raises the value per stored kWh to about 24p. That would give roughly £290/year for 5 kWh and £420/year for 10 kWh — paybacks of about 14 and 13 years. Still not within warranty, but materially closer. **Check which scheme your 15p comes from before deciding.**

## 5. Would an off-peak tariff change the decision?

Yes, but only if the tariff is genuinely cheap. The relevant formula is:

**Net value per kWh delivered = avoided import price − (charge price ÷ round-trip efficiency)**

At 90% efficiency:

| Tariff | Charge price | Discharge/avoided price | Net value per kWh | 5 kWh annual saving | 10 kWh annual saving |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat, solar only | 15p export foregone | 27p | 9.3p | ~£110 | ~£160 |
| Octopus Flux | ~14.6p off-peak | ~30p average peak/day | ~14p | ~£220–240 | ~£340–490 |
| Economy 7 | ~11–13p | ~27p | ~13–15p | ~£190–220 | ~£300–350 |
| Cheap EV-style | ~8p | ~30–32p | ~21–22p | ~£240–280 | ~£500–650 |

The cheap overnight tariff is roughly **twice as valuable per kWh** as storing solar at a 15p export rate. That is the key to making a battery pay — but access is the problem.

### Octopus Flux

Standard Flux is open to new solar-plus-battery customers according to Octopus’s page checked on 29 October 2026, though it has been paused before, so confirm at sign-up [235][175]. It requires solar, a home battery, an MCS document, an export MPAN and a smart meter with half-hourly readings [235].

South-West rates are roughly **14.6p off-peak (02:00–05:00), 24.3p daytime and 34.1p peak (16:00–19:00)** for import, with export at about **4.7p off-peak, 11.1p daytime and 30.3p peak** [601][183][439].

That creates two possible calculations:

**Conservative — Flux only pays for solar-derived exports, not grid-charged exports.** This reflects the SEG rule that suppliers are not obliged to pay for the “brown” element of electricity imported from the grid and re-exported [535][528]. Some suppliers explicitly do not cover stored energy [531]. Under this interpretation, the battery charges from solar first and tops up from the grid only to cover your own evening load. The saving is roughly **£220–240/year for 5 kWh** and **£340–490/year for 10 kWh** — paybacks of about 16–18 years and 11–16 years.

**Optimistic — Flux pays the peak export rate for grid-charged electricity.** Flux’s own description says the battery “can be filled from cheap overnight import and emptied into the grid” at peak [439], and independent worked examples assume grid buy at ~14.6p and peak export at ~25–30p [234][492]. If that is confirmed in writing, the 10 kWh can do a fuller daily cycle and the saving moves towards the top of the £340–490 range. But note the arithmetic: exporting a grid-charged kWh at 30.3p earns less than using it at home to avoid a 34.1p peak import. The export route is only valuable for **spare** capacity, not as a replacement for peak self-use.

The honest conclusion is that **Flux improves the 10 kWh case from “hopeless” to “borderline”, but not to a clear payback.** Even at £490/year and 85% average output, the 10-year saving is about £4,165 against a £5,400 install — still negative before discounting. To recover £5,400 over 10 years at 85% output you need about £635/year, which is above the top of the realistic Flux range.

### A genuinely cheap overnight tariff

If you can get an **8p overnight rate** with a 30–35p daytime/peak rate, the numbers change substantially:

- **5 kWh:** about 4.2 kWh/day × 300–365 days × ~21p net = **£240–280/year**. Payback 14–16 years. The 5 kWh physically cannot cycle enough to do better; its maximum annual arbitrage value is around £320 even at 365 full cycles.
- **10 kWh:** about 8.4 kWh/day × 300–365 days × ~21p net = **£500–650/year**. Payback 8–11 years at £5,400, or 7.5–10 years at £5,000. This is the **only** scenario that gets close to or inside the 10-year warranty.

The catch is eligibility. Octopus Go is sold as an EV tariff, and Octopus’s terms require an electric car or plug-in hybrid charged at home; Intelligent Octopus Go requires a compatible EV or charger [514][517]. Some households report signing up for Go without an EV [503][507], but that is not something to base a £5,000 purchase on. Without an EV, your realistic time-of-use options are Flux (~14.6p) or Economy 7 (~11–13p) [248]. Neither is cheap enough to transform the 5 kWh case, and Flux is only borderline for the 10 kWh.

Consumer-unit work sits outside the relief and is standard-rated at 20% [8]. The deadline is a reason not to delay indefinitely if you have already decided to buy, but it is not a reason to buy a system that does not stack up.

## 7. UK rules and practical costs

**Grid connection.** A battery inverter above 3.68 kW per phase needs G99 pre-approval; the DNO has 45 working days to respond [283][291]. A 5 kW battery inverter added to an existing 4 kWp solar system will usually exceed the G98 limit, so G99 is likely unless the battery is DC-coupled to a hybrid inverter that keeps total export at or below 3.68 kW [287]. Export limitation does not automatically avoid G99; some DNOs accept it, others do not [477]. NGED introduced assessment charges from 1 April 2026 — around £183 including VAT for a standard assessment, with typical domestic G99 costs quoted at £200–£500 and more if reinforcement is needed [451][453]. Ask the installer whether the design will be G98 or G99 before you sign, and check the existing solar connection agreement.

**MCS and SEG.** Battery installation should be certified to MCS **MIS 3012:2025**, and the installer should issue an MCS certificate [575][559]. SEG requires an MCS-certified installation and a smart meter with half-hourly export readings. Adding a battery does not void SEG, but you export less, so SEG income falls [266]. Flux additionally requires a copy of the MCS document and half-hourly readings [235].

**Grid-charged exports.** The SEG rules do not oblige suppliers to pay for electricity that was imported from the grid and re-exported, because the meter cannot distinguish “green” from “brown” electrons [535][528]. Some suppliers exclude stored energy altogether [531]. If you plan to use Flux for overnight grid charging and peak export, get written confirmation from Octopus that those exports will be paid, and keep a record.

**Planning.** A domestic battery is generally treated as permitted development or ancillary equipment, but there is no universally explicit dedicated class in every case [345][343][350]. Check with Bristol City Council if the battery is external, in a conservation area, or on a listed building.

**Fire safety and insurance.** PAS 63100:2024 guidance says batteries should not be in escape routes, hallways, stairwells, lofts or bedrooms; garages, outside walls and suitable utility rooms are preferred [45]. Notify your home insurer and check whether the policy covers battery fires and professional installation.

**Grants and loans.** There is no universal grant for a home battery [366]. The Warm Homes: Local Grant can fund a battery for eligible low-income/EPC D–G households in England, usually alongside solar, and the Warm Homes Loan Scheme offers government-backed lower-interest loans up to £15,000 for a battery — a loan, not a grant [365][379]. Unless you qualify on income/EPC grounds, do not factor a grant into the economics.

### A simple diagnostic

Download 12 months of half-hourly consumption from your smart meter (via your supplier, the Bright app, n3rgy, or an Octopus Home Mini) and 12 months of generation from your inverter app or a CT clamp. Then calculate:

1. **Average daily kWh in four bands:** 02:00–05:00, 05:00–16:00, 16:00–19:00 and 19:00–24:00.
2. **Count the days when surplus exceeds 4.2 kWh** — that is how often a 5 kWh battery can do a full cycle from solar.
3. **Count the days when surplus exceeds 8.4 kWh** — that is how often a 10 kWh battery can do a full cycle from solar.
4. **Compare your evening load with 4.2 and 8.4 kWh.** If your 16:00–24:00 use is consistently below 4 kWh, the 5 kWh is oversized. If it is 7–10 kWh, the 10 kWh is the better fit — but only if it can be filled.
5. **Check whether your 15p is SEG or FIT deemed export.** This single fact can change the answer more than the choice between 5 and 10 kWh.

A useful rule of thumb:

| Evening use (16:00–24:00) | Likely right size for self-consumption |
|---|---|
| Under 4 kWh | 5 kWh may be oversized; consider no battery or a smaller unit |
| 4–6 kWh | 5 kWh |
| 7–10 kWh | 10 kWh, especially with a cheap tariff |
| Over 10 kWh | 10 kWh plus load shifting; consider whether an EV or heat pump is planned later |

## 9. Recommendation

**If your objective is financial return: keep the current setup.** Neither battery pays back within its warranty on the flat 27p/15p numbers, and even the best Flux case is borderline rather than compelling. Confirm your export rate first — if you are on 4–5p rather than 15p, the battery case improves, but not enough to make the 5 kWh compelling.

**If you want a battery for resilience or independence: buy 5 kWh, not 10 kWh.** It covers a useful share of your evening peak, costs £1,500–£2,000 less, and avoids paying for capacity that sits idle in winter. Accept that the financial payback is likely 14–20 years even on a good tariff; the value is in backup power and using more of your own solar, not in the return.

**If you are considering the 10 kWh for arbitrage:** it is the only version that can get near break-even, and only if you can (a) access a genuine ~8p overnight tariff, (b) cycle it almost daily, and (c) buy at the lower end of the installed-cost range. Choose a product with a warranty that suits daily cycling — the Alpha ESS Smile G3 10.1 has unlimited cycles for solar/TOU use, which is a real advantage over the throughput-capped Fox and Sunsynk units [136][135][401]. Confirm in writing that Flux pays for grid-charged exports, confirm the DNO will allow the charge/export profile, and get at least three quotes including G99 and consumer-unit work.

**What I would do next, in order:**

1. **Check your export tariff.** Is the 15p metered SEG, a fixed export deal, or FIT deemed export? This is the single most important unknown.
2. **Download your half-hourly data** and run the four-band diagnostic above.
3. **Get three itemised quotes** for a 5 kWh and a 10 kWh system, including G99, consumer-unit work and any backup wiring.
4. **Ask Octopus, in writing, whether Flux pays the peak export rate for grid-charged electricity** — and whether you can join without an EV.
5. **Check your existing DNO connection agreement** and whether the battery can stay under G98 or needs G99.
6. **Decide what the battery is for.** If it is for backup and self-consumption, 5 kWh is the right size and the payback is a lifestyle cost. If it is for arbitrage, only the 10 kWh + cheap tariff case is worth pursuing — and even then, expect roughly break-even over 10 years before discounting, not a strong return.

The numbers do not support buying either battery as a pure investment at current prices. But they do support a clear distinction: **5 kWh if you want to use more of your own solar; 10 kWh only if you can buy cheap electricity overnight and use it every day.** Without the cheap tariff, the 10 kWh is simply a larger version of a weak solar-only case.

### Material verification gaps

- The annual savings depend on a modelled monthly solar profile and an assumed evening load shape; your actual half-hourly data could move the 5 kWh and 10 kWh savings by roughly ±30%.
- The treatment of grid-charged exports under Octopus Flux is not fully resolved: Octopus’s design and independent guides suggest it is expected, but SEG rules do not oblige payment and some suppliers exclude stored energy. Written confirmation is needed.
- Tesla Powerwall 3 warranty retention is reported inconsistently (70% vs 80% at year 10); it is a larger comparator, not one of the 5/10 kWh options.
- Installed-cost ranges vary widely; the DESNZ/MCS averages are higher than the headline guides, so actual quotes may sit above the figures used here.
- The 15p export assumption may not reflect your actual tariff; if it is FIT deemed export, the economics improve materially.
