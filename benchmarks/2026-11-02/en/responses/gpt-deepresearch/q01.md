# Battery Storage Economics for a 4 kWp Solar Home (Bristol, UK)

A modern home battery (e.g. Tesla Powerwall 3) typically holds 5–14 kWh. In the UK today (Oct 2026) a 5 kWh battery system (including inverter) costs on the order of £3–4 K installed, whereas ~10 kWh systems run ~£6–8 K. For example, Tesla’s 13.5 kWh Powerwall 3 (100% usable, 10 yr warranty) is ~£6,250 plus ~£1.5–2.5 K installation. A 5 kWh Enphase IQ 5P (100% usable, 15 yr warranty) is ~£3,100 + ~£1–1.5 K install, and an AlphaESS 5.04 kWh (usable 4.78 kWh) is ~£2,800 + £1 K. (All these recent figures include supply + install; VAT is 0% on batteries.) Warranties are typically 10 yr (60–70% capacity retained) or longer (Enphase 15 yr). Battery vendors also quote depth-of-discharge (DoD) ~95–100% and round-trip inverter efficiency ~90–97%. 

## Baseline Energy Flows (No Battery)

A 4 kWp solar array generating ~3,600 kWh/year (SW England) supplies ~35% (≈1,260 kWh) directly to meet load, with ~2,340 kWh exported. The household consumes ~4,500 kWh/year (mostly evenings), so about 3,240 kWh are imported. At 27 p/kWh import, the grid cost is 3,240×0.27≈£875. Export at 15 p/kWh yields 2,340×0.15≈£351 credit. Net annual electricity cost ≈£875–£351 ≈£524. In other words, each kWh saved internally is worth 27 p, but each exported kWh foregoes only 15 p. (Typical UK Smart Export Guarantee rates in 2026 range from ~4–12 p, so 15 p is on the high side.) Without a battery, self‐consumption is fixed at ~35% of generation. 

## Battery Energy-Flow Model and Savings

We model annual flows roughly as follows (see assumptions below). A 5 kWh battery (usable ≈4.7 kWh after DoD) can be fully charged on many sunny days by midday surpluses. If it cycles daily, it could store ~1,600 kWh/year (4.7 kWh×≈340 cycles, allowing for some cloudy days). With ~90% round‐trip efficiency, it would deliver ~1,440 kWh/year back to the house. This displaces an equal amount of grid import. Each such kWh avoids a 27 p cost but also costs us one kWh of lost export at 15 p.  Net saved ≈(27×0.9 – 15)≈9.3 p per kWh stored. For ~1,600 kWh stored, annual benefit ≈£150–180. In other words, the 5 kWh battery would cut import by ~1,440 kWh and reduce export by ~1,600 kWh, lowering net cost to ~£524 – ~(1,440×0.27 – 1,600×0.15) ≈ £360 (savings ~£160).  

A 10 kWh battery can capture nearly the entire surplus. If it also cycles daily, it might store ~2,340 kWh (all excess) delivering ~2,000 kWh (with losses). Then import would drop by ~2,000 kWh and export by ~2,340, saving ~(2,000×0.27 – 2,340×0.15) ≈ £190. In practice we found net savings ~£160/yr for 5 kWh and ~£190/yr for 10 kWh (at 27p/15p tariffs). This matches the intuition that each kWh cycled saves only ~10p net. Over 10 years, even ignoring discounting, total savings are ~£1.6–1.9K, far below a £4–8K battery cost. Batteries degrade (warranted to ~60–80% after 10 yr), so later-year savings shrink. 

*Key model assumptions:* 90–95% round-trip efficiency, 95% depth-of-discharge, 10 yr life. The values above scale linearly: if actual cycles are fewer (cloudy year) or more (some summer surplus spillage), savings adjust proportionally. The fixed tariffs (27p import, 15p export) determine that **net** value per kWh stored is ~9–10p (not 12p) when accounting for lost export. Our estimated costs use recent UK prices and typical installation (~£1–2K extra) as cited. Installation costs vary with installer and region, but ~£300–500 per kWh capacity is common (the Tesla and Enphase examples imply ~£1,500–2,500 on top of battery price). 

## 5 kWh vs 10 kWh: Financial Comparison

- **5 kWh battery:** ~£3.5–4.5K installed. Saves ~£160–180/yr (as above). ≈40–50 yr simple payback, far beyond 10 yr warranty.  
- **10 kWh battery:** ~£7–8K installed (e.g. ~£6.25K for 13.5 kWh supply + £2K install, or two 5 kWh Enphase units ~£6.2K + £2K) giving ~£190/yr saving. ~40–50 yr payback again.  

In both cases the economics are very weak on raw numbers. Even if lifetime is 15 yr (some warranties), the amortized saving per year (~£130–150 after battery degradation) is much less than the ~£400–800/yr “return” one might hope for. (For comparison, if export were *all* lost, and import 27p, max possible is (27–15)×3600×0.95≈£430; our calculation yielded much less because only surplus can be stored.)  

## Impact of Off-Peak (Time-of-Use) Tariffs

A separate effect is time-of-use arbitrage. Some UK tariffs (e.g. Economy 7/10 or Octopus Go) sell cheap night power (~10–12 p/kWh) and expensive day power (~27 p). A battery could charge from the grid at 11 p overnight and discharge in evening at 27 p, saving ~16 p per kWh (minus losses) instead of the ~10 p we got from solar storage. In theory, a 10 kWh battery could then avoid ~10 kWh of peak imports each day at cost ~£4.40 (10×16p) – about £1,600/yr. However, in practice this requires fully discharging the battery each day into the evening peak load. In our case, the daily usage (~12.3 kWh) is similar to battery size, but since we also have solar, managing state-of-charge is complex: charging fully at night means the battery can’t absorb midday sun. The actual benefit depends on whether evening loads can be covered without sacrificing daytime solar usage. In summary, off-peak tariffs *could* significantly improve returns if the battery is used mainly for cheap-night/high-day arbitrage. But with “mostly evening” consumption and only moderate daytime solar, the gain is limited unless habits or loads shift. It may be worth recalculating with a specific tariff, but rough effect is boosting savings by a few hundred £/yr if battery goes to grid on peak and refills at night.

## Profile Sensitivity and Caveats

Our analysis used annual sums and a few assumptions. In reality, half-hourly profiles matter: for instance, if midday generation often far exceeds 5 kWh, a 5 kWh battery will be full by midday and spill more to grid, so its marginal benefit is slightly lower. Conversely, if some winter days have surplus less than 5 kWh, both 5 kWh and 10 kWh batteries might under-utilize capacity. If household demand is flatter (some daytime use), then more of the 3,600 kWh could be self-used without battery, reducing the battery’s incremental benefit. Off-peak benefits likewise depend on load timing. The SEG/export rules also matter: we assumed all generated-but-not-used energy is paid at 15p; some tariffs pay less (3–6p) for excess, which would actually increase battery value by that difference. Finally, future tariff changes or incentives (e.g. capacity payments) could alter the math. But under current 27p/15p rates, **the conclusions follow directly from the arithmetic**: a ~£4K battery only saves ~£160/yr, and a ~£8K battery saves ~£190/yr. The specific savings per kWh (≈9–10p) come from the given prices, while the scalability (5 vs 10) depends on how much excess solar there is to store. 

**Conclusion:** At today’s prices and our home’s load, adding even a 5–10 kWh battery barely changes the annual energy balance and yields a very long payback. The numbers alone suggest *no*, it’s not financially sensible to install a battery just for bill savings – the current setup likely saves more per pound (e.g. by lowering consumption or demand). The battery might still be considered for non-monetary reasons (backup power, grid services, or future-proofing), but numerically the modest ~£160–190/yr gain for thousands of pounds spent makes it unattractive. An off-peak tariff could improve the case somewhat, but only if it enables truly cost-effective arbitrage. All these conclusions are robust given the stated prices and tariffs; actual half-hourly data would fine-tune the numbers, but not change the main outcome that the modest export credit (15p) and large battery cost dominate the result.
